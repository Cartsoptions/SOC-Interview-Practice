# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-10-13

**Curriculum:** Questions 125–128 | Phishing and business email compromise, email authentication, Microsoft Defender XDR hunting, and account-compromise response.  
**Evidence standard:** Observation → Hypothesis → Confirmed Evidence. All domains, users, IP addresses and events below are simulated.

## Question 125

### Interview Question

An accounts-payable employee reports an email that appears to come from a regular supplier. It asks them to replace the supplier's bank details before paying an overdue invoice. How would you triage the report, and what is the difference between ordinary phishing and business email compromise?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat the request as a potential business email compromise, or BEC, because it uses trusted business context to influence a payment. Phishing is the broader use of deceptive electronic messages to obtain access, information or an action. BEC is a business-focused fraud pattern that often impersonates or compromises an executive, employee or supplier account to redirect money or sensitive information. A BEC message may contain no malicious link or attachment, so a clean attachment scan does not make the payment request safe.

I would preserve the original message and its headers, identify the visible From address, envelope sender, Reply-To, sending infrastructure, authentication results, subject, recipients and message identifiers. I would compare the request with previous legitimate supplier correspondence, checking for a new Reply-To address, changed writing or payment instructions, unusual urgency and a lookalike domain. I would use the email-security platform to determine who else received the message and whether it was delivered, blocked or removed.

I would ask the finance or supplier-management owner to verify the bank-detail change through a trusted secondary channel using contact details already held by the organisation, not a telephone number or link in the suspicious email. If the email came from a genuine supplier domain, I would still consider supplier-account compromise. I would not contact the suspected attacker from the email thread or approve a payment.

**Observation:** A supplier-branded email requests changed bank details and urgent payment.  
**Hypothesis:** The message may be a lookalike-domain impersonation or may originate from a compromised supplier mailbox.  
**Confirmed Evidence:** Examples include the supplier denying the request, a newly registered/lookalike domain, unauthorised mailbox activity, or corroborating evidence that the sender account was compromised. Suspicion alone is not proof that the supplier account was breached.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The message contains no attachment and all URLs are benign, but the Reply-To points to `supplier-payments.example` instead of the supplier's established domain. The supplier confirms by a known telephone number that no bank change was requested. I would classify the message as a confirmed fraudulent payment attempt, scope all recipients and coordinate authorised removal and blocking. If money was already sent, I would escalate immediately to incident leadership, finance, the bank and the organisation's fraud-reporting process.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Original message and complete headers, not only a screenshot or forwarded copy
- From, MAIL FROM/Return-Path, Reply-To, Message-ID and Received path
- SPF, DKIM, DMARC and platform verdicts, without treating a pass as proof of business legitimacy
- Supplier-domain history, first-contact status and previous approved payment-change process
- Other recipients, delivery locations and related messages in the campaign
- Whether anyone replied, disclosed information, changed payment details or transferred funds
- Trusted out-of-band verification and urgent escalation if financial action occurred

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365 Threat Explorer and the Email entity page, Proofpoint TAP/URL Defense where deployed, Abnormal Security where deployed, Exchange message trace, SIEM, DNS/domain intelligence, finance records and incident tickets.

### Interview Takeaway

"I would treat an unexpected bank-detail change as potential BEC even if there is no malicious link. I would preserve the original email, validate the sender and payment request through an independent trusted channel, scope recipients and escalate immediately if funds or credentials may be at risk."

## Question 126

### Interview Question

You review this simulated email evidence for a payment-change request:

```text
From: Accounts Team <accounts@supplier.example>
Return-Path: <bounce@supplier.example>
Reply-To: payments@secure-invoice.example
Authentication-Results: spf=pass; dkim=pass header.d=supplier.example; dmarc=pass
Subject: Updated bank account for invoice 4817
```

Does `dmarc=pass` prove that the request is legitimate? Explain your investigation.

### Interview-Ready Answer

No. A DMARC pass means that at least one supported authentication mechanism passed and aligned with the domain in the visible From address. It helps assess domain-level authentication, but it does not validate the truth of the payment request, the sender's intent or the security of the mailbox.

In this example, SPF, DKIM and DMARC passing makes simple spoofing of `supplier.example` less likely. It does not rule out a compromised supplier account, an abused authorised sending service or a socially engineered message sent through legitimate infrastructure. The different Reply-To domain is a strong investigation lead because responses would be directed away from the authenticated From domain, but it is not automatically malicious; some organisations legitimately use separate reply-handling services.

I would inspect the full header chain and message details, compare the Reply-To and sending pattern with earlier legitimate supplier messages, review the age and reputation of `secure-invoice.example`, and ask the supplier through a trusted known contact to validate the request. I would also scope related messages and check whether recipients replied or took action.

**Observation:** The message passed SPF, DKIM and DMARC, while Reply-To uses another domain.  
**Hypothesis:** A legitimate account or mail service may be abused, or the Reply-To may redirect a fraudulent conversation.  
**Confirmed Evidence:** Requires supplier validation and supporting technical or account evidence. Authentication results alone neither confirm fraud nor prove the business request is genuine.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Threat Explorer shows that the same sender previously used no Reply-To header and discussed payments only within an established thread. The new domain was first seen today, and the supplier's finance contact denies the bank change. I would escalate the message as confirmed BEC-related fraud, remove matching messages according to authority, preserve evidence and investigate whether any users responded or paid.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Alignment between the visible From domain and the authenticated SPF or DKIM domain
- Full Authentication-Results and Received headers from the trusted receiving system
- Reply-To, Return-Path, Message-ID domain and sender IP
- Historical sender-recipient relationship and normal supplier workflow
- Domain age/reputation as supporting context, not a verdict by itself
- Recipient replies, payment changes, link clicks and credential submission
- Supplier confirmation through independently sourced contact details

### Tools / Logs

Defender for Office 365 Email entity page and Threat Explorer, Microsoft Message Header Analyzer, Proofpoint or Abnormal evidence where deployed, Exchange message trace, DNS/WHOIS intelligence, SIEM and finance/change records.

### Interview Takeaway

"DMARC pass confirms aligned domain authentication, not that the person or business request is trustworthy. I would investigate Reply-To differences, historical behaviour, account compromise and independent supplier confirmation before reaching a verdict."

## Question 127

### Interview Question

An analyst has a suspicious message's Microsoft 365 `NetworkMessageId`. Explain what this Microsoft Defender XDR advanced-hunting query does, how you would use its results and what it cannot prove.

```kusto
let TargetNetworkMessageId = "SIMULATED-NETWORK-MESSAGE-ID";
EmailEvents
| where Timestamp > ago(7d)
| where NetworkMessageId == TargetNetworkMessageId
| project EmailTime=Timestamp, NetworkMessageId, SenderFromAddress,
          SenderMailFromAddress, RecipientEmailAddress, Subject,
          DeliveryAction, DeliveryLocation, ThreatTypes,
          AuthenticationDetails
| join kind=leftouter (
    UrlClickEvents
    | where Timestamp > ago(7d)
    | where Workload == "Email"
    | project ClickTime=Timestamp, NetworkMessageId, AccountUpn,
              Url, ActionType, IsClickedThrough,
              ClickThreatTypes=ThreatTypes, IPAddress
) on NetworkMessageId
| order by ClickTime desc
```

### Interview-Ready Answer

The query sets a target message identifier, searches `EmailEvents` for that message during the last seven days and selects email-delivery, sender, recipient, threat and authentication fields. It then uses a left-outer join on `NetworkMessageId` to add matching Safe Links click events from `UrlClickEvents`. A left-outer join keeps the email record even when no click record matches. The results can show the recipient, delivery location and action, along with a recorded click's user, URL, click action, click-through indicator, threat verdict and source IP.

I would use the output to scope which mailbox received the message and whether Microsoft Defender for Office 365 recorded a related Safe Links click. I would then pivot to the Email entity page or Threat Explorer for the full message and URL context, and to identity, proxy, DNS and endpoint evidence if credential theft or payload execution is suspected.

The query does not prove that the user entered credentials, downloaded a file or was compromised. A click may have been blocked. Even an allowed click does not by itself prove successful exploitation. Conversely, no matching row does not prove that nobody visited the site: `UrlClickEvents` represents Safe Links clicks in supported workloads/apps, and Microsoft documents cases where message metadata cannot be joined using `NetworkMessageId`.

**Observation:** Defender records an email and possibly one or more associated Safe Links click events.  
**Hypothesis:** A recipient may have interacted with a phishing URL.  
**Confirmed Evidence:** Requires evidence of the resulting action, such as credential submission followed by unauthorised sign-in, malicious file execution, or user confirmation corroborated by logs.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The query returns a delivered message and an `ActionType` showing an allowed click by the accounts-payable user. I would inspect the URL chain and threat verdict at click time, then investigate Entra sign-ins, device/network telemetry and the user's account activity. If the user confirms entering credentials and an unfamiliar session appears shortly afterward, I would escalate as likely account compromise rather than closing the case as only a phishing click.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Correct table availability, licence, retention window and exact `NetworkMessageId`
- Delivery action/location and latest message state in the Defender portal
- Click time, user, `ActionType`, `IsClickedThrough`, URL chain, threat type and IP
- Other recipients and similar messages or URLs
- Entra sign-ins, MFA activity, session/device context and mailbox audit events
- Proxy, DNS and endpoint telemetry for non-Safe-Links or payload activity
- Evidence gaps before assigning a verdict

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender XDR advanced hunting, Defender for Office 365, Threat Explorer, Sentinel where integrated, Entra sign-in logs, Microsoft Purview audit, EDR, proxy and DNS logs.

### Interview Takeaway

"The query correlates one Microsoft 365 email with recorded Safe Links clicks by `NetworkMessageId`. It helps scope delivery and interaction, but a click is not proof of compromise and no click row is not proof that the URL was never visited."

## Question 128

### Interview Question

A finance user receives a supplier-themed login link. Defender records an allowed Safe Links click. Fifteen minutes later, Entra shows a successful sign-in from an unfamiliar network, a new inbox rule moves messages containing “invoice” to RSS Feeds, and the mailbox sends payment-change emails to customers. How would you investigate, decide the verdict and respond within Tier 1 authority?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat this as a high-priority suspected account-takeover and BEC incident because the evidence forms a coherent sequence:

**Phishing delivery → URL click → unusual authentication → email-hiding rule → fraudulent outbound messages**

I would first validate timestamps, time zones, the target user and message identifiers. I would inspect the phishing message, URL chain and click evidence, then review the unfamiliar sign-in's IP, location, autonomous system, device, client, authentication details and session context. A successful MFA result would not automatically make the sign-in legitimate because attackers can abuse stolen sessions or other authentication flows; I would rely on the complete evidence and trusted user confirmation.

Next, I would review the mailbox rule's creation time, actor/session and conditions, and compare these with the suspicious sign-in. MITRE ATT&CK identifies malicious rules that move or delete messages as T1564.008, Email Hiding Rules. I would inspect sent mail, message trace and related recipients to determine the scope of fraudulent messages, and look for forwarding, delegates, OAuth grants, MFA changes and additional affected users or sessions.

**Observation:** A phishing email, allowed click, unfamiliar successful sign-in, new hiding rule and fraudulent outbound messages occurred close together.  
**Hypothesis:** The user entered credentials or an attacker obtained a valid session, then used the mailbox for BEC while hiding replies.  
**Confirmed Evidence:** User denial of the sign-in/rule/messages plus correlated identity and audit records would support confirmed account compromise. The precise initial-access mechanism may remain uncertain unless credential, token or device evidence establishes it.

I would preserve the message, sign-in, click, mailbox-audit and outbound-message evidence and urgently escalate under the account-compromise playbook. If authorised, responders should block or disable access as appropriate, revoke active sessions, reset credentials, review MFA methods, remove malicious rules/forwarding/delegates, contain fraudulent messages and investigate affected recipients. I would perform only actions allowed to Tier 1 and document who authorised each containment step. I would also ensure finance/fraud teams validate transactions and use the organisation's bank and reporting procedures if payments were attempted.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The user confirms they entered their password on the supplier-themed page but did not create the inbox rule or send the payment emails. Audit records associate the rule and outbound messages with the unfamiliar session. I would classify this as confirmed mailbox compromise with BEC impact, immediately escalate for authorised identity/mailbox containment, scope every recipient and preserve the timeline for recovery and lessons learned.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Original phishing message, recipients, delivery state, URLs and click evidence
- Entra sign-in, authentication, MFA, device, IP, location, session and risk context
- Trusted user confirmation and known VPN/mobile-travel explanations
- Purview/Exchange audit evidence for inbox rules, forwarding, delegates and sent/deleted mail
- OAuth applications, newly registered MFA methods and other persistence changes
- Outbound recipients, customer replies, payment or data exposure and related accounts
- Evidence preservation, severity, escalation, authorised containment and recovery ownership

### Tools / Logs

Defender for Office 365 and Defender XDR, Entra ID sign-in/audit logs, Microsoft Purview audit, Exchange Online message trace and mailbox rules, Sentinel or Splunk, Abnormal account-takeover evidence where deployed, Proofpoint TAP where deployed, EDR, proxy/DNS and finance/fraud systems.

### Interview Takeaway

"I would correlate the phishing message, click, sign-in, mailbox-rule and outbound-mail evidence. If the user denies the actions and the audit trail links them to the unfamiliar session, I would escalate as confirmed account compromise with BEC impact and coordinate authorised session revocation, credential reset, mailbox clean-up, message containment and financial response."

## Verification Notes

- Microsoft documents the `EmailEvents` fields used here, including `NetworkMessageId`, sender/recipient, delivery, threat and authentication details: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/defender-xdr/advanced-hunting-emailevents-table
- Microsoft documents `UrlClickEvents` as Safe Links click telemetry and lists `AccountUpn`, `Workload`, `NetworkMessageId`, `ActionType`, `IsClickedThrough`, URL, threat and IP fields. It also documents join limitations for some Drafts/Sent-items clicks: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/defender-xdr/advanced-hunting-urlclickevents-table
- Microsoft documents Threat Explorer/Real-time detections, email/URL investigation and correlation using Network Message ID: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/defender-office-365/threat-explorer-email-security
- RFC 7489 states that DMARC passes when an authenticated SPF or DKIM identifier passes and aligns with the RFC5322.From domain; it does not validate the business truth of message content: https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7489
- UK NCSC describes business payment fraud/BEC and recommends rapid reporting to IT and direct contact with the bank if payment occurred: https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/section/respond-recover/business-payment-fraud
- FBI/IC3 guidance recommends verifying payment or account-detail changes through a secondary channel or known contact information: https://www.ic3.gov/CrimeInfo/BEC
- Microsoft documents investigation and remediation of compromised Microsoft 365 email accounts, including sign-in review, session revocation, password reset, forwarding and hidden inbox-rule review: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/defender-office-365/responding-to-a-compromised-email-account

- MITRE ATT&CK T1566 describes phishing as Initial Access; T1564.008 describes email hiding rules: https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/ and https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1564/008/
- Proofpoint's official TAP SIEM API documentation states that telemetry can include blocked/permitted URL Defense clicks and blocked/delivered messages containing threats: https://help.proofpoint.com/Threat_Insight_Dashboard/API_Documentation/SIEM_API
