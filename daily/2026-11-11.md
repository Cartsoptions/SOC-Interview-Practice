# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-11-11

**Curriculum:** Questions 205–208 | Supplier-invoice BEC triage, email-authentication reasoning, Defender XDR hunting, and compromised-mailbox incident response

## Question 205

### Interview Question

Accounts Payable reports an urgent supplier email asking for bank details to be changed before a large invoice is paid. The display name and visible address look familiar, and the message appears in an existing conversation. How would you triage this as a Tier 1 analyst?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat the request as potential business email compromise, or BEC: social engineering that abuses a business identity or a compromised mailbox to cause payment, data or account actions. I would not approve the bank change, reply in the suspicious thread or assume that a familiar display name and conversation history prove legitimacy.

In a SOC environment, I would first preserve the message, headers and message identifiers, then record the recipient, claimed supplier, invoice, requested change, amount, deadline and whether anybody clicked, replied or initiated payment. I would compare the visible `From` address, envelope sender/Return-Path, Reply-To, sending infrastructure and SPF/DKIM/DMARC results, while remembering that passing authentication does not prove the business request is genuine.

I would use the email-security portal to check delivery action, detections, URLs, attachments, similar messages and other recipients. I would compare the sender domain with the known supplier domain, look for lookalike spelling, first-contact or unusual correspondence, and review prior trusted correspondence through an independent source. Finance or the supplier owner should verify the request using a previously known telephone number or established approval channel—not contact details supplied in the message.

If the message came from the supplier's genuine infrastructure, possibilities include a compromised supplier mailbox or malicious use by an authorised sender. If it came from an internal mailbox, I would escalate immediately for account-compromise scoping. Tier 1 should preserve and correlate evidence, stop the business process through the approved fraud procedure, and escalate; email removal, sender blocking, account containment and payment recall are performed only by authorised teams under the playbook.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The email asks Accounts Payable to replace a long-standing supplier's bank account. The message passed authentication for the supplier's real domain, but the supplier confirms by a known phone number that it did not request the change. Mailbox evidence later shows that the supplier account was compromised and the attacker replied inside a genuine invoice thread.

- **Observation:** A familiar supplier identity requests an urgent, high-impact payment change in an existing thread.
- **Hypothesis:** The message may be lookalike-domain fraud, spoofing, or abuse of a compromised legitimate mailbox.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Independent supplier verification rejects the request, and the supplier's investigation confirms unauthorised mailbox access and thread use.

The verdict is **high-severity true positive — supplier-mailbox BEC targeting payment redirection; internal recipients and payment status require immediate scoping**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve the original message, full headers, Internet/Network Message ID where available and UTC timestamps.
2. Record the claimed supplier, recipient, amount, invoice, requested change and urgency.
3. Establish whether anyone replied, clicked, disclosed credentials, changed records or initiated payment.
4. Compare visible From, envelope sender/Return-Path, Reply-To, domains and authentication results.
5. Review URLs, attachments, delivery action/location, policy verdicts and similar messages.
6. Check prior trusted correspondence and whether this is first contact or an unusual sender-recipient pattern.
7. Have Finance/supplier management verify through a known independent channel.
8. Search for the sender, subject, URLs, attachment hashes and message identifiers across mailboxes.
9. Escalate for authorised message removal, indicator restriction, account containment and fraud/payment response.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365/Defender XDR or Proofpoint, Threat Explorer/email investigation, message trace, full email headers, `EmailEvents`, `EmailUrlInfo`, `EmailAttachmentInfo`, Entra sign-in and audit logs, mailbox audit/inbox-rule evidence, Sentinel or Splunk, finance approval records, supplier master-data history and case notes.

### Key Interview Takeaway

A familiar address, thread and authentication pass do not prove a payment request is genuine. I would preserve the message, verify the request out of band, scope delivery and user action, and escalate quickly through both security and fraud procedures.

## Question 206

### Interview Question

The suspected supplier-fraud email shows SPF, DKIM and DMARC as pass. The hiring manager asks, “Does that clear the email?” How would you answer, and what evidence would you examine next?

### Interview-Ready Answer

No. An authentication pass means specific domain-use checks succeeded; it does not certify the sender's intent, the truth of the request or the safety of the content.

SPF checks whether the sending system is authorised for the SMTP identity, normally the envelope `MAIL FROM` domain used by DMARC. DKIM verifies a cryptographic signature associated with the signing domain. DMARC passes when at least one passing SPF or DKIM authenticated identifier aligns, in relaxed or strict mode, with the domain in the visible author `From` address. RFC 9989 states that a DMARC pass indicates only that use of the Author Domain was validated as authorised by its domain owner.

Therefore, a criminal can register a lookalike domain, configure SPF/DKIM/DMARC correctly and send fully authenticated fraud. A genuine supplier or executive mailbox can also be compromised, causing messages to pass authentication for the real domain. Authentication failure is useful evidence, but authentication success is not a benign-content verdict.

I would compare the visible From, envelope sender, DKIM signing domain and Reply-To; examine alignment and the receiving system's authentication results; and investigate domain similarity, message history, unusual recipients, tone, urgency and requested action. I would then correlate message delivery, URL/attachment evidence, sender and recipient account activity, mailbox rules, forwarding, sign-ins and independent business-owner verification.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The visible From and DKIM signing domain are the supplier's genuine domain and DMARC passes. The request still changes bank details without the normal dual-authorisation process. Supplier verification rejects the request, and its identity logs identify an unauthorised session before the message was sent.

- **Observation:** SPF, DKIM and DMARC pass for the genuine supplier domain.
- **Hypothesis:** The mailbox or an authorised sending workflow may have been compromised despite successful authentication.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Independent supplier denial plus unauthorised sign-in and send activity confirms abuse of the legitimate account.

The verdict is **true-positive BEC from a compromised legitimate mailbox; the authentication pass correctly describes domain authorisation but does not clear the message**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve the receiving system's authentication results and full headers.
2. Identify visible From/Author Domain, envelope MAIL FROM domain, DKIM `d=` signing domain and Reply-To.
3. Determine which SPF or DKIM path produced the DMARC pass and whether alignment was relaxed or strict where the evidence exposes it.
4. Compare the domain with the known supplier domain and check for lookalike characters or recently introduced domains.
5. Review sender-recipient history, first-contact context, tone and deviation from the normal payment process.
6. Examine URLs, attachments, delivery verdicts and similar messages.
7. Correlate mailbox, sign-in, audit, forwarding and inbox-rule evidence when the account is in scope.
8. Verify the request through a known business channel and document the result.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Raw message headers, Microsoft Defender for Office 365/Defender XDR `EmailEvents`, Threat Explorer, Proofpoint message evidence, DNS SPF/DKIM/DMARC records, Entra sign-in logs, Exchange mailbox audit and rule/forwarding evidence, Sentinel/Splunk and supplier-verification records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Email authentication answers whether a domain identity was authorised and aligned; it does not answer whether the person, request or content is trustworthy.

## Question 207

### Interview Question

Write a safe first-pass Microsoft Defender XDR Advanced Hunting query to find delivered inbound, first-contact messages where the visible From domain differs from the envelope MAIL FROM domain. Explain what the output can and cannot prove.

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would use the documented `EmailEvents` fields and keep the first pass explainable:

```kusto
EmailEvents
| where Timestamp >= ago(7d)
| where EmailDirection == "Inbound"
| where DeliveryAction == "Delivered"
| where IsFirstContact == 1
| where isnotempty(SenderFromDomain)
    and isnotempty(SenderMailFromDomain)
| where SenderFromDomain != SenderMailFromDomain
| project Timestamp,
          NetworkMessageId,
          SenderDisplayName,
          SenderFromAddress,
          SenderFromDomain,
          SenderMailFromAddress,
          SenderMailFromDomain,
          RecipientEmailAddress,
          Subject,
          AuthenticationDetails,
          DeliveryAction,
          DeliveryLocation,
          ThreatTypes,
          EmailActionPolicy,
          UrlCount,
          AttachmentCount
| order by Timestamp desc
```

Microsoft documents `SenderFromDomain` as the visible From domain, `SenderMailFromDomain` as the envelope/Return-Path domain, `IsFirstContact` as whether the message is the first contact, and `AuthenticationDetails` as a string containing authentication verdicts. The table depends on Microsoft Defender for Office 365 data availability.

The query returns a prioritised lead set: delivered inbound first-contact messages with differing visible and envelope domains. That difference can occur legitimately with mailing platforms, forwarding and third-party services. It does not prove DMARC failed, the sender spoofed a brand or the message is BEC. I would inspect `AuthenticationDetails` rather than inventing an undocumented structure, verify domain alignment from supported evidence, and correlate URLs, attachments, sender reputation, message history, business context and related recipients. I would baseline approved senders and tune only after investigation.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The query returns an invoice-change email whose visible domain resembles a supplier, while its envelope domain belongs to an unrelated bulk-mail service. It is first contact, contains a reply address on another domain and reached three finance users. The real supplier rejects the request.

- **Observation:** A delivered first-contact message has mismatched visible/envelope domains and targets finance recipients.
- **Hypothesis:** The mismatch may support a lookalike-domain BEC attempt, but could also reflect legitimate third-party mail delivery.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Header analysis, domain comparison, message content, independent supplier denial and related-recipient findings confirm the fraudulent campaign.

The verdict is **high-severity true positive — multi-recipient supplier-impersonation BEC; no payment or credential submission is yet confirmed**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Confirm Defender for Office 365 coverage, retention and the intended time window.
2. Open the raw `EmailEvents` rows and preserve Network Message IDs and timestamps.
3. Inspect visible From, envelope sender, Reply-To and authentication results.
4. Determine whether a legitimate mail service explains the difference.
5. Compare domains for lookalike spelling and check sender-recipient history.
6. Pivot to `EmailUrlInfo` and `EmailAttachmentInfo` using supported identifiers.
7. Search related recipients, subjects, sender domains, URLs and attachments.
8. Check user actions and independent supplier/business-owner verification.
9. Document false-positive possibilities, coverage limits and the evidence supporting the verdict.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender XDR Advanced Hunting, `EmailEvents`, `EmailUrlInfo`, `EmailAttachmentInfo`, Threat Explorer, Defender for Office 365, raw headers, message trace, Entra/mailbox audit evidence, Sentinel/Splunk and case-management records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

The query identifies explainable BEC leads; domain mismatch and first contact are risk signals, not verdicts. Authentication, content, relationships and independent verification establish meaning.

## Question 208

### Interview Question

You confirm that an internal finance mailbox was compromised. The attacker created a forwarding/inbox rule, monitored a real invoice thread, sent changed bank details and a payment was released. How would you contain, scope, eradicate and support recovery as Tier 1?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would classify this as a high-severity or critical BEC incident with confirmed account compromise, persistence, internal message abuse and financial loss. I would immediately preserve the original messages and headers, message identifiers, sign-in and audit records, send activity, mailbox rules and forwarding configuration, authentication-method changes, OAuth/app consent, session/token evidence, deleted or moved messages, finance approvals and the payment timeline.

Under the approved incident playbook, I would urgently escalate to the incident lead, identity/email administrators, Finance/fraud, Legal/privacy and the bank-contact process. Microsoft recommends disabling the affected account during investigation, revoking active sessions, reviewing MFA methods and user-consented applications, and reviewing/removing suspicious forwarding and inbox rules. Those are authorised administrative actions; Tier 1 should not independently reset executive/finance accounts, purge mailboxes or contact a bank outside the documented process.

Containment should stop further account access and payment activity: authorised teams disable or restrict the account, revoke sessions/tokens, reset credentials and compromised authentication methods, remove malicious rules/forwarding, remove fraudulent messages and restrict confirmed malicious infrastructure. Finance should contact the bank immediately through established fraud channels to request recall or hold, but recovery of funds is not guaranteed.

Scoping must identify the initial-access time and method; every suspicious sign-in/session, MFA change, OAuth grant and mailbox rule; messages read, moved, deleted, forwarded or sent; all recipients and replies; other compromised identities or endpoints; data accessed in email, OneDrive, SharePoint or Teams; delegated mailbox access; and whether credentials or payment details were reused elsewhere.

Eradication and recovery include removing persistence, rotating credentials and affected secrets, restoring mailbox configuration and legitimate messages, validating clean endpoints where relevant, re-enabling the user only after identity control is restored, monitoring for recurrence, reconciling supplier/payee records and completing regulatory/contractual assessment. NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 supports preserving incident records, validating incident magnitude, coordinated containment, secure recovery and lessons learned.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Investigation finds an unfamiliar successful sign-in followed by a new forwarding rule that hid replies from the supplier. The attacker sent replacement bank details from the genuine finance mailbox, and a transfer was made. Authorised responders disable the account, revoke sessions, remove the rule and coordinate an urgent bank recall. The affected payment and data exposure remain tracked as separate impact streams.

- **Observation:** A finance account has suspicious access, a forwarding/inbox rule, fraudulent send activity and a completed payment.
- **Hypothesis:** The attacker may retain sessions or OAuth persistence, have accessed sensitive correspondence and have targeted additional recipients.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Identity and mailbox audit evidence proves unauthorised access, rule creation and message activity; finance records prove the fraudulent transfer.

The verdict is **critical true positive — confirmed internal-mailbox BEC with persistence and financial loss; wider identity, data and recipient exposure must be scoped**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve emails, headers, message IDs, audit/sign-in records, rules, forwarding, consent and payment evidence.
2. Build a UTC timeline from initial access through rule creation, thread monitoring, fraudulent send and payment.
3. Escalate immediately to incident, identity/email, Finance/fraud, Legal/privacy and bank-response owners.
4. Support authorised account disablement, session/token revocation, credential/authentication-method reset and rule removal.
5. Review suspicious sign-ins, devices, locations, MFA changes, OAuth grants, roles and delegated access.
6. Scope messages read, forwarded, deleted, moved or sent and every internal/external recipient.
7. Hunt related sender addresses, domains, URLs, message IDs, rules and sign-in infrastructure across the tenant.
8. Check OneDrive, SharePoint, Teams and endpoints for related access or compromise.
9. Track financial recovery and data-exposure workstreams separately; do not assume a payment recall will succeed.
10. Validate mailbox and identity recovery before re-enablement, then monitor for renewed activity.
11. Feed lessons into payee-change verification, dual authorisation, anti-forwarding controls, detection and user reporting.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365/Defender XDR, Entra sign-in and audit logs, Exchange mailbox audit and inbox-rule/forwarding evidence, Unified Audit Log, message trace/Threat Explorer, OAuth consent and authentication-method records, endpoint EDR, Sentinel/Splunk, finance/payee-change and banking records, case-management and legal/privacy records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Confirmed BEC is both a security incident and a fraud event. Preserve identity, mailbox, message and payment evidence; contain through authorised owners; scope persistence, data and recipients; and support rapid financial response without promising recovery.

## Verification Notes

- RFC 9989 states that DMARC pass requires a passing SPF or DKIM identifier aligned with the visible Author Domain, and that a pass indicates only that use of that domain was authorised. It also explains why an SPF or DKIM pass alone can belong to a bad actor's domain: https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9989.html
- Microsoft documents the `EmailEvents` fields used in Question 207, including `Timestamp`, `NetworkMessageId`, visible and envelope sender fields/domains, recipient, subject, direction, delivery action/location, threat/policy fields, `AuthenticationDetails`, URL/attachment counts and `IsFirstContact`: https://learn.microsoft.com/defender-xdr/advanced-hunting-emailevents-table
- Microsoft describes `AuthenticationDetails` as a string containing pass/fail verdicts for DMARC, DKIM, SPF or composite authentication. The query therefore projects it for inspection and does not assume an undocumented parsed schema: https://learn.microsoft.com/defender-xdr/advanced-hunting-emailevents-table
- Microsoft documents `EmailUrlInfo` and `EmailAttachmentInfo` as the advanced-hunting tables for URLs and attachments in email, enabling supported pivots from email investigations: https://learn.microsoft.com/defender-xdr/advanced-hunting-emailurlinfo-table and https://learn.microsoft.com/defender-xdr/advanced-hunting-emailattachmentinfo-table
- Microsoft's compromised-account response guidance recommends disabling the account during investigation, revoking active sessions, reviewing MFA methods and user-consented applications, and reviewing/removing suspicious forwarding and inbox rules: https://learn.microsoft.com/defender-office-365/responding-to-a-compromised-email-account
- Microsoft's phishing investigation playbook recommends correlating email activity with Entra sign-ins, audit and endpoint evidence, investigating suspicious sign-ins, OAuth consent/token abuse and mailbox activity, then removing messages, containing accounts, revoking sessions/tokens and recovering securely: https://learn.microsoft.com/security/zero-trust/security-operations-playbook-phishing
- Microsoft documents suspicious inbox-manipulation/forwarding rules as common post-compromise techniques in BEC and phishing that can move, delete, mark read or forward messages: https://learn.microsoft.com/defender-xdr/alert-grading-playbook-inbox-manipulation-rules and https://learn.microsoft.com/defender-xdr/alert-grading-playbook-inbox-forwarding-rules
- Proofpoint's official Threat API documents `Impostor` as a BEC threat classification and exposes message identifiers and sender evidence for investigation; exact fields and workflows depend on the licensed product and deployment: https://help.proofpoint.com/Threat_Insight_Dashboard/API_Documentation/Threat_API
- NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3, finalised in April 2025, recommends preserving incident records/evidence, validating magnitude, coordinating containment, securely recovering and feeding lessons into improvement: https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/61/r3/final
