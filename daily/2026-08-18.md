# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-08-18

## Question 4

### Interview Question

What is a firewall, and how would you use firewall evidence during a SOC investigation?

### Interview-Ready Answer

A firewall is a security control that regulates network traffic between networks or hosts according to security policy. In practical terms, it decides which traffic is allowed, denied, or otherwise handled based on rules and context. NIST describes firewalls as devices or programs that control network traffic between systems with different security postures.

In a SOC environment, I would not think of a firewall only as something that blocks traffic. I would also use its logs as an investigation source. Firewall telemetry can help show which source and destination IPs communicated, which ports or protocols were involved, whether a connection was allowed or denied, when it occurred, and sometimes which rule or application was associated with the decision, depending on the product and configuration.

As a Tier 1 analyst, I would normally investigate the evidence and follow the organisation's playbook rather than independently changing production firewall policy unless I am specifically authorised. A blocked connection can still matter because it may reveal scanning or repeated attack attempts, while an allowed connection may need further investigation if it is unexpected.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

A SIEM alert shows repeated outbound connections from an employee workstation to an unfamiliar external IP over an unusual port. I would check the firewall or network-security logs to confirm whether the traffic was allowed or denied, identify frequency and timing, and correlate that with DNS, proxy and EDR telemetry. If EDR shows that an unexpected process initiated the connection and threat intelligence indicates the destination is malicious, I would escalate with the correlated evidence. I would not conclude that the host is compromised merely because a firewall log contains an unfamiliar IP.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Source and destination IP addresses
- Source and destination ports and protocol
- Direction of traffic and timestamps
- Allow, deny, reset or other recorded action, depending on product terminology
- Firewall rule/policy context where available
- Repetition, volume and whether other hosts show the same pattern
- DNS/proxy records associated with the destination
- EDR process or user responsible for the connection where available
- Whether containment or a block request is authorised by the incident playbook

### Tools / Logs

Firewall logs, SIEM such as Splunk or Microsoft Sentinel, EDR such as Microsoft Defender for Endpoint, DNS logs, proxy logs, network telemetry and threat-intelligence sources.

### Interview Takeaway

A firewall controls network traffic according to security policy. In the SOC, I would use firewall logs to understand who communicated with whom, over what service, whether the traffic was allowed or denied, and then correlate that evidence with endpoint, DNS, proxy and identity data before deciding whether escalation is required.

## Question 5

### Interview Question

What is the difference between an IDS and an IPS, and what would that difference mean to you as a SOC analyst?

### Interview-Ready Answer

An IDS, or Intrusion Detection System, monitors system or network activity for signs of possible malicious or unauthorised behaviour and generates alerts. An IPS, or Intrusion Prevention System, has detection capabilities and can also attempt to stop detected activity. NIST's current glossary describes an IDS as software that automates intrusion detection, while an IPS can detect intrusive activity and attempt to stop it.

Operationally, the important SOC difference is the action taken. An IDS alert tells me suspicious activity was observed; it does not necessarily mean the traffic was blocked. With an IPS, I must check whether prevention actually occurred and exactly what action the product recorded. I would not assume an IPS alert means the attack succeeded, and I would not assume an IDS alert means compromise occurred. In both cases I need context and corroborating evidence.

As a Tier 1 analyst, my role is usually to validate the alert, understand the source, destination, signature or behaviour, determine whether the activity reached or affected the target, correlate with other telemetry, document findings, and escalate when warranted. Rule tuning or changing prevention policy is generally an engineering or administrator responsibility unless my role explicitly includes it.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

An IDS/IPS alert reports a possible web exploit attempt against an internet-facing application server. I would first identify whether the security device only detected the attempt or actively blocked/dropped it. I would review the source IP, destination server, signature, timestamp and repeated activity. Then I would correlate with WAF, web-server, EDR and application logs. If the IPS says the traffic was blocked and there is no evidence the payload executed, that reduces confidence of compromise, but repeated attempts may still justify escalation or blocking decisions. If application or EDR evidence shows successful exploitation, the case becomes an incident regardless of the original IDS/IPS disposition.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Whether the control was operating as IDS/detection or IPS/prevention for the event
- Alert/signature or behavioural detection name and severity
- Source IP, destination IP, destination service and timestamp
- Recorded action such as alert, drop, block or reset, using the product's actual terminology
- Whether the target was vulnerable or exposed to the attempted technique
- Related firewall, WAF, web, EDR and SIEM events
- Evidence of successful execution, persistence, account activity or outbound connections
- Whether similar alerts affected additional hosts

### Tools / Logs

IDS/IPS telemetry, SIEM, firewall/WAF logs, EDR, server/application logs, DNS/proxy logs and vulnerability-management data.

### Interview Takeaway

IDS primarily detects and alerts; IPS can also attempt to prevent. As a SOC analyst, I would confirm what the device actually did and then investigate whether the suspicious activity had any effect on the target instead of treating the alert itself as proof of compromise.

## Question 6

### Interview Question

What is a VPN, and how would you investigate suspicious VPN access as a Tier 1 SOC analyst?

### Interview-Ready Answer

A VPN, or Virtual Private Network, creates a protected logical connection over an existing network so users, hosts or sites can communicate securely across networks such as the Internet. NIST describes VPNs as virtual networks built on top of existing physical networks that can provide a secure communications mechanism. Microsoft also notes that user VPNs commonly provide remote connectivity to enterprise resources and normally require authentication.

In a SOC environment, VPN activity is valuable identity and remote-access evidence. I would look at successful and failed authentication attempts, the account involved, source IP and approximate location, device or client information where available, MFA results, session start/end times, assigned VPN address, and what the user accessed after connecting. I would be careful with geolocation: Microsoft explicitly notes that IP-to-physical-location mapping is best-effort and that VPNs and mobile providers can make locations misleading.

If I see a successful VPN login from an unusual location, I would treat it as a hypothesis requiring investigation rather than immediate proof of account compromise. I would compare it with the user's normal behaviour, MFA and identity signals, endpoint information, concurrent sessions, travel context and post-login activity.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

A SIEM alert shows repeated failed VPN authentication attempts for one account followed by a successful connection from an IP not previously associated with the user. I would review the VPN authentication logs and identity-provider sign-in data, confirm MFA outcome, check whether the IP is associated with known corporate infrastructure or a privacy/VPN service, look for impossible or unusual session overlap, and investigate what happened after login. If the account then accesses sensitive systems or shows abnormal endpoint or identity activity, I would escalate according to the playbook. I would not disable the account solely because the country lookup appears unusual unless the organisation's authorised procedure calls for immediate containment.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Failed and successful VPN authentication attempts and timing
- Username/account sensitivity or privilege level
- Source IP and location as contextual, not definitive, evidence
- MFA result and authentication method where available
- Device/client information and session duration where available
- Concurrent or overlapping sessions
- Normal user baseline and known travel/remote-work context
- Identity-provider sign-in events and risk signals
- Post-login access to internal resources
- Related EDR, firewall, DNS and proxy activity
- Escalation or containment steps authorised by the incident playbook

### Tools / Logs

VPN concentrator or remote-access logs, Microsoft Entra sign-in logs where applicable, SIEM, MFA/NPS logs where deployed, firewall logs, EDR, DNS/proxy logs and ticketing/incident-management systems.

### Interview Takeaway

A VPN provides protected remote or site-to-site connectivity over an existing network. In a SOC investigation, I would use VPN and identity logs to determine who connected, from where, how they authenticated, and what they did next, while treating IP geolocation as supporting context rather than proof.

## Verification Notes

- NIST's CSRC glossary defines a firewall as a device or program that controls the flow of network traffic between networks or hosts with differing security postures. NIST SP 800-41 Rev. 1 provides firewall-policy guidance.
- NIST's CSRC glossary defines an IDS as software that automates intrusion detection and an IPS as a system that can detect intrusive activity and attempt to stop it. NIST SP 800-94 remains the final NIST guide; its 2012 Revision 1 draft was retired in 2022 and never became final.
- NIST's VPN glossary and SP 800-77 guidance describe VPNs as logical/virtual networks built over existing networks that provide protected communications. NIST published SP 800-77 Rev. 1 in 2020 for IPsec VPN guidance.
- Microsoft Learn documents that point-to-site VPNs provide remote connectivity to enterprise networks and typically require user authentication. Microsoft Entra/NPS guidance documents VPN-related authentication logging where that architecture is used.
- Microsoft Learn warns that IP-address geolocation in Entra sign-in logs is not definitive because VPNs and mobile providers can issue addresses from remote pools.
