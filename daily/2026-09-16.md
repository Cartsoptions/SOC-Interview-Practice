# SOC Interview Practice - 2026-09-16

## Question 67

### Interview Question
A SIEM alert shows that a user was added to a privileged Windows security group. What do Events 4728 and 4732 mean, and how would you investigate whether the change is legitimate or attacker-driven privilege escalation/persistence?

### Interview-Ready Answer
Windows Security Event **4728** records a member being added to a security-enabled **global** group, while **4732** records a member being added to a security-enabled **local** group. Microsoft notes that the Subject identifies the account requesting the change, the Member identifies what was added, and the Group identifies the target group. The Subject Logon ID can help correlate the change with a logon such as Event 4624.

The event is an observation, not proof of compromise. Administrators and IAM processes legitimately modify groups. MITRE ATT&CK maps adversary additions to local or domain groups to **T1098.007, Additional Local or Domain Groups**, which can support persistence or privilege escalation.

**Observation:** an account or machine was added to a security group.

**Hypotheses:** approved provisioning, temporary privileged access, automation, administrator error, or attacker account manipulation.

**Confirmed malicious change:** requires evidence that the addition was unauthorised or was followed by malicious privileged activity.

In a SOC environment, I would identify who made the change, which principal was added, how sensitive the group is, whether the change was approved, and what the newly privileged identity did afterwards. I would prioritise groups such as Administrators, Domain Admins or Remote Desktop Users according to the organisation's critical-group list.

### Real-World SOC Scenario
A domain controller records Event 4728 showing a normal help-desk user added to a highly privileged global group outside the approved change window. The Subject account belongs to an administrator, but the administrator does not recognise the action. Minutes later, the newly privileged user authenticates to a server it does not normally access.

**Observation:** unexpected privileged-group membership was granted.

**Hypothesis:** a compromised administrator identity may have modified group membership to elevate or preserve access.

I would correlate **administrator logon -> group change -> new member authentication -> endpoint/remote activity**. If there is no authorised request and suspicious activity follows, I would escalate promptly according to the identity incident playbook.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst
- Event timestamp and domain controller/host
- 4728 versus 4732 and the actual group scope
- Subject account/SID and Subject Logon ID
- Member account/SID
- Target group and business sensitivity
- Related Event 4624/session context where available
- Approved IAM/PIM/change request
- Whether the Subject account normally manages that group
- Recent account creation/password/reset activity
- Authentication by the newly privileged member
- RDP, SMB, WinRM or administrative activity afterwards
- EDR/process evidence around the change where available
- Similar privileged-group changes elsewhere
- Tier 1 containment/escalation authority

### Relevant Tools / Logs
Windows Security Events 4728, 4732 and correlated authentication/account-management events; Active Directory audit data; Microsoft Sentinel; Splunk; Wazuh; Defender for Endpoint or another EDR; IAM/PIM/change-management records.

### Key Interview Takeaway
Events 4728 and 4732 tell me that membership changed, not that an attacker definitely escalated privileges. I would validate the actor, member, target group, authorisation and subsequent privileged behaviour before reaching a verdict.

## Question 68

### Interview Question
Your vulnerability scanner reports two findings: one has a CVSS score of 9.8 but is on an isolated low-value test system, while another has a lower CVSS score but appears in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on an internet-facing production server. Which would you prioritise, and why?

### Interview-Ready Answer
I would **not prioritise from CVSS alone**. CVSS communicates vulnerability severity; it is not a complete organisational risk score. FIRST's CVSS v4.0 framework includes Base, Threat, Environmental and Supplemental metric groups, with Base scores from 0 to 10. Environmental and threat context can refine the picture.

CISA describes its **Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) Catalog** as an authoritative source of vulnerabilities known to have been exploited in the wild and recommends using KEV as an input to vulnerability-management prioritisation.

So the lower-CVSS vulnerability on an internet-facing production asset may deserve the higher remediation priority because there is evidence of real-world exploitation plus meaningful exposure and business impact. I would still validate applicability, affected version, compensating controls and vendor guidance rather than assuming every scanner finding is exploitable.

**Observation:** scanner findings with different severity and exposure contexts.

**Hypotheses:** either finding may represent the greater organisational risk depending on exploitability, exposure, asset criticality and controls.

**Confirmed priority:** comes from validated vulnerability + asset/exposure context + threat evidence + business impact + remediation requirements.

### Real-World SOC Scenario
A weekly scan finds a critical-severity vulnerability on a powered-down lab server and a high-severity vulnerability on an internet-facing production gateway. The second CVE is in CISA KEV and the asset is reachable from the internet.

I would validate both, but I would raise the KEV/exposed production finding urgently because active exploitation evidence and external exposure materially increase risk. I would check vendor remediation, whether exploitation indicators are present, whether compensating controls reduce exposure, and then track remediation and validation with the vulnerability/engineering team.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst
- CVE and affected product/version
- Scanner confidence and possible false positive
- CVSS version/vector, not only the number
- CISA KEV status
- Vendor advisory and patch/mitigation
- Internet-facing versus internal/isolated exposure
- Asset criticality and data/service impact
- Exploit prerequisites and reachable attack path
- Existing EDR/WAF/firewall/segmentation controls
- Threat intelligence and exploitation evidence
- Signs the asset may already have been targeted/compromised
- Remediation owner, SLA and exception status
- Rescan/validation after remediation
- Escalation requirements for actively exploited or critical assets

### Relevant Tools / Logs
Vulnerability scanners; asset/CMDB inventory; CISA KEV; vendor advisories; CVE/NVD context where appropriate; EDR; SIEM; firewall/WAF/proxy logs; patch-management and ticketing systems.

### Key Interview Takeaway
CVSS tells me severity, not the whole business risk. I would prioritise using validated exploitability, KEV/active exploitation, exposure, asset criticality, compensating controls and business impact, then verify remediation with a rescan or equivalent validation.

## Question 69

### Interview Question
How has NIST's current incident-response guidance changed from the older 'Preparation, Detection and Analysis, Containment/Eradication/Recovery, Post-Incident Activity' model, and how should a Tier 1 SOC analyst describe the current lifecycle in an interview?

### Interview-Ready Answer
The current authoritative NIST publication is **SP 800-61 Revision 3**, finalised in April 2025. It **supersedes SP 800-61 Revision 2** and integrates incident response into **NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0** rather than treating incident handling as an isolated four-phase loop.

NIST explains that all six CSF 2.0 Functions play roles in incident response: **Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover**. Govern, Identify and Protect support preparation and broader risk reduction; Detect, Respond and Recover cover discovering, managing and recovering from incidents; lessons learned and improvement feed back across the organisation.

So in a current interview I would not present the Rev. 2 four-phase lifecycle as if it were still NIST's latest model. I can mention it as the older framework if asked, then explain the current Rev. 3/CSF 2.0 approach.

For Tier 1 operations, this does not mean triage, escalation, containment, eradication and recovery disappear. Those operational activities still matter, but they sit within a wider risk-management framework. My L1 responsibility would usually centre on detecting/validating alerts, gathering evidence, determining scope/severity, documenting, escalating, and performing authorised response actions according to playbooks.

### Real-World SOC Scenario
An EDR alert shows suspicious credential access followed by lateral movement attempts.

**Observation:** endpoint and authentication telemetry indicate suspicious activity.

**Hypothesis:** an endpoint/account compromise may be progressing laterally.

I would validate the alert, preserve the timeline, scope affected identities/devices, assess urgency and escalate. If the playbook authorises a Tier 1 containment action such as endpoint isolation, I would execute it; otherwise I would escalate immediately for authorised containment. Response teams would then continue eradication/recovery as appropriate, and lessons learned should feed improvements to controls, detections and procedures.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst
- Alert source, confidence and supporting telemetry
- Affected user/device/service and business criticality
- Timeline and initial-access indicators
- Process, identity and network evidence
- Scope across other hosts/accounts
- Whether malicious activity is ongoing
- Severity and escalation criteria
- Evidence preservation/documentation requirements
- Approved containment actions within Tier 1 authority
- Handoff information for Tier 2/IR
- Recovery/monitoring evidence when the incident returns for validation
- Lessons or detection gaps to capture after closure

### Relevant Tools / Logs
SIEM such as Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk; EDR such as Defender for Endpoint; Windows/Linux logs; identity logs; firewall/proxy/DNS; email-security telemetry; ticket/case-management system; NIST SP 800-61r3 and organisational incident-response playbooks.

### Key Interview Takeaway
NIST SP 800-61r3 is the current guidance and aligns incident response with CSF 2.0's Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover Functions. As Tier 1, I would translate that framework into disciplined alert validation, evidence gathering, scoping, documentation, escalation and only authorised containment actions.
