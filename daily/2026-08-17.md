# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-08-17

## Question 1

### Interview Question

A user reports a suspicious invoice email asking them to click a link and urgently change a supplier's bank details. How would you investigate it as a Tier 1 SOC analyst, and what would make you consider Business Email Compromise (BEC)?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat the email as suspicious but not immediately call it BEC. My first job would be to preserve and review the available evidence: sender and reply-to details, message routing/authentication information, URLs or attachments, recipients, delivery status, and whether other users received similar messages. In Microsoft Defender for Office 365, where licensing and permissions allow, Threat Explorer/Real-time detections and the Email entity page can provide detailed message and related-entity information for investigation.

I would then determine whether the case is only an external phishing attempt or whether there are signs that a real mailbox has been compromised. Indicators that increase concern for BEC/account compromise include suspicious inbox or forwarding rules, unusual sent/deleted messages, unexplained external forwarding, suspicious sign-in activity, or legitimate-looking messages sent from a compromised account. Microsoft specifically documents BEC in the context of compromised Microsoft 365 email accounts and recommends investigating the affected identity and associated services.

I would also check whether the user clicked the link, entered credentials, opened an attachment, approved MFA, or made a payment. If there is evidence of credential theft, account takeover, malicious execution, or financial fraud, I would escalate immediately according to the organisation's incident process. I would not rely on a single indicator such as SPF/DKIM/DMARC or a display name to make the final decision; those are pieces of evidence to correlate.

MITRE ATT&CK maps phishing to T1566 and distinguishes, among other sub-techniques, Spearphishing Attachment (T1566.001) and Spearphishing Link (T1566.002).

### Real-World SOC Scenario

An accounts-payable employee receives an email that appears to come from a known supplier. The message says an invoice is overdue and provides a link to 'review updated bank details.' The display name looks correct, but the analyst finds that the message came from a look-alike domain. Threat Explorer shows the same message was delivered to several employees, and one user clicked the URL. The analyst would investigate the clicked user's identity and endpoint activity, block/remediate the malicious message or indicators using authorised procedures, and escalate if compromise or payment fraud is suspected.

If the organisation uses Proofpoint, relevant telemetry may include URL/attachment threat information and forensic evidence available through its Targeted Attack Protection capabilities. If it uses Abnormal Security, account-takeover investigations may include behavioural anomalies involving logins, locations, MFA methods, mail-rule changes, and email activity. Exact actions depend on the organisation's product, licence, configuration, permissions, and playbooks.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Sender, return/reply-to, sending domain and message-routing/authentication evidence
- Embedded URLs, redirects, attachment names/types and known threat verdicts
- Whether the message reached other recipients and whether anyone clicked/opened/submitted credentials
- Microsoft Defender for Office 365 Email entity / Threat Explorer evidence where available
- Identity sign-ins, MFA events and risky/unusual authentication context
- Inbox rules, forwarding configuration and suspicious Sent/Deleted Items if account compromise is suspected
- Endpoint/EDR, DNS, proxy and browser/network telemetry after a click or attachment execution
- Whether a financial request or bank-detail change requires immediate business/fraud escalation

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365, Microsoft Defender XDR/Endpoint, Entra ID sign-in logs, SIEM, Proofpoint TAP where deployed, Abnormal Security where deployed, DNS/proxy logs, browser/network telemetry and endpoint logs.

### Interview Takeaway

I would not decide that an email is BEC from appearance alone. I would investigate the message, user actions, identity activity and endpoint/network evidence, determine whether an account or business process was compromised, and escalate/remediate according to the organisation's playbook.

## Question 2

### Interview Question

What is the current NIST approach to incident response, and how would you apply it during a SOC investigation?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would explain that NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3, published in April 2025, supersedes Rev. 2 and integrates incident response into NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 risk management. The current guidance emphasises incident detection, response and recovery while also showing that preparation and continuous improvement depend on the broader CSF 2.0 functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover.

In a SOC shift, that means I would not treat incident response as only a rigid checklist that begins after compromise. Governance, asset knowledge and preventive controls affect how well we respond. Once suspicious activity is detected, my Tier 1 role is to validate the alert, collect and correlate evidence, document scope and severity, escalate according to the playbook, and support authorised response actions. Recovery and lessons/improvement feed back into future detection and prevention.

I would also be careful in an interview not to present the older Rev. 2 four-phase model as NIST's current framework. It is still useful historical context, but Rev. 3 is the current publication.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

An EDR alert reports suspicious PowerShell activity on a finance workstation. I would validate the alert using process, user, parent-process, command-line and network context; determine whether there is related activity on other systems; document what is observed versus inferred; and escalate if malicious activity is supported. Response teams may isolate the host or disable an account according to authority and playbooks. After containment and eradication, recovery verifies normal operation, while lessons learned can improve detections and controls.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Detection source and the evidence that triggered the alert
- Affected user, host, process, IP/domain and timeline
- Related EDR, identity, SIEM, DNS/proxy and authentication activity
- Scope: one host/user or broader activity
- Business impact and escalation criteria
- Which containment actions I am authorised to perform versus actions requiring escalation
- Documentation needed for response, recovery and later improvement

### Tools / Logs

SIEM, EDR, Windows/Linux logs, identity logs, DNS/proxy/firewall telemetry, ticketing/incident-management platform and organisation-specific playbooks.

### Interview Takeaway

Current NIST guidance treats incident response as part of overall cybersecurity risk management. In the SOC I would validate and scope the incident, support authorised response and recovery, document evidence, and feed lessons back into better controls and detections.

## Question 3

### Interview Question

Walk me through vulnerability management as it is actually performed in an organisation rather than just defining a vulnerability scanner.

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would describe vulnerability management as a continuous risk-reduction process, not a one-time scan. A practical workflow is to maintain asset visibility, identify vulnerabilities, validate and prioritise findings based on technical severity plus business/exposure context, assign remediation or mitigation, and verify that the fix actually worked. Patching is one important remediation method, but not every vulnerability is resolved only by installing a patch.

NIST SP 800-40 Rev. 4 describes enterprise patch management as identifying, prioritising, acquiring, installing and verifying patches, updates and upgrades. That verification step is important: a ticket marked 'patched' is not enough evidence by itself. Organisations commonly rescan or otherwise confirm that the vulnerable condition is gone and track exceptions where remediation cannot happen immediately.

As a Tier 1 SOC analyst, vulnerability management may not be my primary ownership area, but the data is extremely useful during alert triage. If an alert targets a server known to have an unremediated internet-exploitable vulnerability, that can significantly raise investigation priority and help me explain the risk during escalation.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

A scanner reports a critical vulnerability on an internet-facing web server. I would not treat the CVSS score alone as proof that the server is compromised. The vulnerability team would validate the finding, assess exposure and business criticality, determine remediation or compensating controls, patch/test according to change procedures, and verify the fix. If the SOC simultaneously sees exploitation attempts against that server, I would correlate the vulnerability information with WAF/firewall, EDR, application and network telemetry and escalate based on evidence of attempted or successful exploitation.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Correct asset/owner and whether the finding is genuine
- Internet exposure and business criticality
- Vulnerability/CVE details and technical severity
- Exploitability/threat intelligence and observed exploitation attempts where available
- Existing compensating controls
- Remediation owner, target date and exception status
- Evidence that the patch/mitigation was successfully applied
- Rescan or other verification that the vulnerable condition is removed

### Tools / Logs

Vulnerability scanners, asset inventory/CMDB, patch-management platform, SIEM, EDR, firewall/WAF logs, threat intelligence and ticketing/change-management systems.

### Interview Takeaway

Vulnerability management is continuous: identify, prioritise, remediate or mitigate, and verify. As a SOC analyst I use vulnerability context to improve alert prioritisation, but I still need evidence before concluding that a vulnerable system has actually been exploited.
