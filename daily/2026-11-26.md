# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-11-26

**Curriculum:** Questions 249–252 | QR-code phishing triage, vulnerability-remediation validation, Windows process-chain interpretation, and pre-impact ransomware response

## Question 249

### Interview Question

A user reports an email containing a QR code that claims their Microsoft 365 session will expire today. The message has no clickable link or attachment, and the email gateway did not block it. How would you investigate it as a Tier 1 SOC analyst?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat the QR code as a way of moving the destination out of the normal clickable-link path, not as proof that the message is malicious. My first objective is to preserve the original message and determine the sender, delivery path, authentication results, business context, QR destination and user interaction.

In Microsoft Defender for Office 365, where licensed and permitted, I would use the Email entity page or Threat Explorer to review the sender, return path, delivery action, headers, authentication, URLs, attachments and related messages. Microsoft documents that the Email entity page exposes plain-text headers and SPF, DKIM, DMARC and composite-authentication results. Those results help establish whether the sending domain authenticated; they do not establish that the request or QR destination is safe.

I would decode or detonate the QR content only through the organisation's approved analysis tooling, not by scanning it with a personal phone. I would examine the destination domain, redirects, registration and reputation as enrichment, then search for matching sender, subject, QR image characteristics or destination across other recipients. If Proofpoint or Abnormal Security is deployed, I would review its available message, identity, supplier or behavioural evidence without assuming features or automated actions that are not enabled in that tenant.

I would contact the user to establish whether they scanned the code, entered credentials, approved MFA or downloaded anything. If they interacted, I would correlate Entra sign-in and risk data, device telemetry and subsequent mailbox activity. Tier 1 can preserve and scope evidence, assign a supported verdict, and escalate. Message removal, account/session containment or blocking should follow the playbook and delegated permissions.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The displayed sender resembles the IT helpdesk, but the message originated from a newly registered external domain. The QR code redirects to a Microsoft-themed credential page. Threat Explorer finds 26 delivered copies, and one recipient confirms entering a password. Entra sign-in logs then show a successful unfamiliar sign-in followed by a new inbox rule.

- **Observation:** A session-expiry email uses a QR code and bypassed initial gateway detection.
- **Hypothesis:** The QR code leads to credential phishing and at least one account may be compromised.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** The decoded redirect reaches a credential-harvesting page; the user confirms password submission; the unfamiliar sign-in and unauthorised inbox rule confirm account misuse.

The verdict is **confirmed QR-code credential phishing with one confirmed Microsoft 365 account compromise; scope all recipients, escalate authorised identity and mailbox containment, and preserve the evidence timeline**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Original message, message ID, sender, return path, reply-to, originating infrastructure and headers.
2. SPF, DKIM, DMARC and composite-authentication results as identity evidence, not a safety verdict.
3. QR destination and redirect chain using approved analysis tooling.
4. Domain age, reputation and threat-intelligence matches as enrichment.
5. Other recipients and similar messages in Threat Explorer, Proofpoint or Abnormal Security where deployed.
6. User interaction: scanned, visited, submitted credentials, approved MFA or downloaded content.
7. Entra sign-ins, risk signals, MFA changes and session activity.
8. Mailbox rules, forwarding, sent mail and other post-compromise actions.
9. Endpoint and network telemetry from the user's device.
10. Authorised message containment, identity response, escalation and user communication.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365 Email entity and Threat Explorer; Proofpoint or Abnormal Security where deployed; message headers; approved QR and URL analysis; Microsoft Entra sign-in and audit logs; Defender for Endpoint; DNS, proxy and firewall logs; incident-management records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

A QR code is a delivery mechanism, not a verdict. I preserve the email, analyse the destination safely, scope recipients, establish user interaction and correlate identity and mailbox evidence before escalating.

## Question 250

### Interview Question

A vulnerability scanner previously reported a critical flaw on an internet-facing appliance. The infrastructure team says it is fixed because a firewall rule now restricts access, but the next authenticated scan still detects the vulnerable software version. How would you validate remediation and close, or refuse to close, the finding?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would distinguish **remediated**, **mitigated** and **still susceptible**. A patch or supported upgrade may remove the vulnerable condition. A firewall restriction may reduce exposure without removing it. The scanner's continued detection is evidence that the underlying version remains present, but I would still validate asset identity, scan recency, credential success, plugin output and vendor guidance before assigning the final status.

I would confirm the CVE and affected-version range against the vendor advisory, check whether the CVE is in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, and record asset criticality, internet exposure, exploit activity and compensating controls. CVSS represents technical severity; it should not be treated as a complete organisational risk score. FIRST recommends adding threat and environmental context, while CISA recommends prioritising known exploited vulnerabilities and other evidence of active exploitation.

Next, I would review the change record and exact firewall rule: allowed sources, protocols, management paths, IPv4 and IPv6 coverage, alternate interfaces and whether the device can still be reached through VPN, partner or internal routes. I would request an authorised validation scan after the change and check the logs or packet evidence that demonstrates the control is functioning. I would not perform intrusive exploitation unless the organisation's procedure explicitly authorises it.

If the vulnerable version remains but effective compensating controls reduce access, I would document the finding as **mitigated, not remediated**, including the control owner, residual risk, expiry or review date and permanent-fix plan. Closure should follow the vulnerability-management policy and risk-acceptance authority, not a Tier 1 analyst's unsupported judgement.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The asset and scanner credentials are valid. The appliance version remains vulnerable and the CVE appears in CISA KEV. The new firewall rule blocks general internet access but still permits a broad third-party support range, and appliance logs show connection attempts from that range. No confirmed exploitation evidence is found.

- **Observation:** The vulnerable version remains, while exposure has been narrowed but not eliminated.
- **Hypothesis:** The firewall change reduced likelihood but did not fully mitigate an actively exploited vulnerability.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Authenticated scanning confirms the affected version; KEV confirms exploitation in the wild; rule review and logs confirm a remaining external path. No evidence yet confirms compromise of this appliance.

The verdict is **not remediated and not ready for normal closure; escalate urgent permanent remediation, review the remaining access path and investigate for compromise indicators without claiming exploitation occurred locally**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Correct asset, product, version and CVE mapping.
2. Authenticated-scan success, signature freshness and finding evidence.
3. Vendor advisory, fixed version and supported mitigation.
4. CISA KEV status and current threat intelligence.
5. Internet, VPN, partner, IPv6 and alternate-interface exposure.
6. Firewall rule scope, hit logs and bypass paths.
7. Asset criticality, data handled and business dependency.
8. Evidence of attempted or successful exploitation.
9. Validation scan results and change record.
10. Residual risk, control owner, review date and permanent remediation plan.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Authenticated vulnerability scanner; CISA KEV; vendor advisory; CVE/CVSS data; CMDB and asset inventory; firewall configuration and logs; IDS/IPS, WAF, VPN and appliance logs; change, exception and risk-acceptance records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Reduced exposure is not automatically remediation. I validate the underlying condition and the compensating control, record residual risk and close only under the organisation's defined authority.

## Question 251

### Interview Question

Splunk shows Windows process-creation telemetry in this order on a finance laptop: Outlook starts PowerShell; PowerShell runs with a long encoded argument; a signed Windows utility then loads a DLL from the user's temporary directory; DNS and proxy logs show an immediate connection to a newly observed domain. Explain what is observation, hypothesis and confirmed evidence, and how you would triage it.

### Interview-Ready Answer

The process chain is highly suspicious, but I would not label the DLL as malware solely from filenames or parent-child relationships. Event ID 4688 records creation of a new process when Audit Process Creation is enabled. Microsoft also documents that the process command line is empty by default unless command-line inclusion is separately enabled. Splunk field names depend on the input and field extractions, so I would inspect the raw event and source type before relying on a field such as `CommandLine` or `ParentProcessName`.

I would validate the timestamps, host, user, process IDs, creator process, executable paths and command lines. I would determine whether Outlook opened a message or attachment at that time, decode the PowerShell content in an isolated approved workflow, and collect the DLL's hash, signer, creation source and prevalence without executing it. A signed Windows binary can be abused to run malicious content, so the signature of the loader does not make the loaded DLL trustworthy.

I would pivot to Defender for Endpoint or another EDR for the full process tree, file events, network connections, detections and remediation status. Microsoft's Defender device timeline supports chronological event review and parent-child process-tree investigation. I would correlate DNS and proxy evidence, search the hash, domain, DLL path and process pattern across the estate, and check whether the activity matches an approved finance add-in or automation.

If evidence confirms malicious execution or command-and-control, Tier 1 should escalate immediately and request or perform containment only within delegated authority. I would preserve the initial email and endpoint timeline because they may establish initial access, execution and scope.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The raw 4688 events confirm the process chain and command-line logging was enabled. The decoded script retrieves the DLL from the same domain seen in proxy logs. Defender shows the DLL loaded and a successful outbound session before remediation; the hash is detected as malicious by the organisation's EDR, and two other hosts received the same email.

- **Observation:** Outlook spawned encoded PowerShell, followed by DLL execution from a temporary directory and an outbound connection.
- **Hypothesis:** A malicious email triggered script-based execution and command-and-control.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** The decoded retrieval path, malicious EDR verdict, DLL load and successful network session confirm malware execution. Related emails on two hosts expand the incident scope, but execution on those hosts must be verified separately.

The verdict is **confirmed malware execution and outbound communication on the finance laptop, with two additional exposed hosts requiring immediate investigation and authorised containment**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Raw event source, source type, timestamp and field extraction quality in Splunk.
2. Event ID 4688 process ID, creator process, path, user and command line where enabled.
3. Outlook message, attachment and user interaction around the first event.
4. Safely decoded script content and download destination.
5. DLL hash, signer, path, source, prevalence and EDR verdict.
6. EDR process tree, file operations, persistence and remediation status.
7. DNS, proxy and firewall evidence for the destination and session.
8. Same hash, domain, email and process sequence elsewhere.
9. Approved software, finance add-ins and change records.
10. Evidence preservation, severity, containment authority and escalation handover.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Splunk; Windows Security Event ID 4688; Defender for Endpoint device timeline and process tree; Sysmon where configured; Outlook and Microsoft Defender for Office 365 evidence; DNS, proxy and firewall logs; approved reputation or malware-analysis services; change records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

A suspicious process chain supports a hypothesis, not an automatic verdict. I validate raw telemetry, safely analyse the script and file, correlate endpoint and network evidence, then scope and escalate confirmed execution.

## Question 252

### Interview Question

Defender for Endpoint alerts that PowerShell attempted to disable recovery features on a file server. The command was blocked, but the same account created a new service on another server and several endpoints are making SMB connections to shared storage. No files are encrypted yet. How would you prioritise and respond as Tier 1?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat this as a high-severity suspected pre-impact ransomware incident, not close it because one command was blocked. MITRE ATT&CK maps attempts to remove or disable recovery mechanisms to T1490, Inhibit System Recovery. PowerShell execution is T1059.001, but ATT&CK mappings describe observed behaviour; they do not identify the actor or prove ransomware by themselves.

My immediate goal is to determine whether the activity is isolated, contained or still spreading. I would establish the alert time, account, source and target devices, exact block status, service-creation evidence, SMB destinations and whether mass file changes, backup access or security-control tampering occurred. I would correlate the Defender incident, device timelines, identity logs, Windows process and service telemetry, file-server audit evidence where available, firewall or SMB telemetry and change records.

The attempted recovery inhibition increases urgency because it can precede destructive impact. In the Cyber Kill Chain, the organisation may be at execution, installation or actions-on-objectives depending on confirmed evidence; I would use that model to communicate progress, not as a substitute for evidence. In the Pyramid of Pain, hashes and IPs may support rapid blocking, but behaviours such as recovery tampering, remote service creation and abnormal SMB fan-out are generally harder for an adversary to change and more useful for resilient detection.

Tier 1 should declare or escalate the suspected major incident according to the playbook, preserve evidence and initiate authorised containment. That may include isolating affected endpoints, disabling or restricting the compromised account, protecting shared storage and backup systems, and blocking confirmed indicators—but only within delegated permissions and with operational owners involved. I would not wait for encryption before escalating.

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 aligns incident response with the CSF 2.0 functions. Here, Detect means analysing the related activity; Respond includes coordinated containment and communication; Recover should begin only after the threat is contained and restoration integrity is validated. Lessons learned should feed improvements in controls and playbooks.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Defender confirms the recovery command was blocked on one server, but the same account successfully created services on three other servers. File-server logs show rapid writes and renames from one affected host, and the backup console records an unauthorised deletion attempt that failed. There is no approved change.

- **Observation:** Recovery tampering, remote service creation, SMB fan-out and abnormal file activity occur under one account.
- **Hypothesis:** Compromised credentials are being used to deploy ransomware before broad encryption.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Successful remote service creation, abnormal file writes and the unauthorised backup-deletion attempt confirm coordinated malicious activity. The available evidence supports a ransomware-like pre-impact intrusion, while the specific ransomware family remains unconfirmed.

The verdict is **confirmed active multi-host compromise with imminent destructive-impact risk; trigger major-incident escalation and authorised containment immediately rather than waiting for encryption**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Exact Defender alert, action and remediation status on every device.
2. Account privilege, authentication source, logon sessions and other accounts involved.
3. PowerShell parent process, command line and related script activity.
4. Service creation and remote-execution evidence on peer servers.
5. SMB sources, destinations, shares and abnormal fan-out.
6. File creation, rename, deletion or extension-change activity.
7. Backup, snapshot and recovery-system access or tampering.
8. Security-control disabling and persistence.
9. Asset criticality, business services and containment dependencies.
10. Major-incident declaration, evidence preservation, authorised containment and handover.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender XDR and Defender for Endpoint; Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk; Windows process, service and authentication logs; Sysmon where configured; file-server auditing; SMB and firewall telemetry; identity logs; backup-console logs; CMDB, change and incident-management records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

One blocked command does not prove containment. Recovery tampering plus remote services, SMB fan-out and abnormal file activity warrants immediate major-incident escalation before encryption begins.
