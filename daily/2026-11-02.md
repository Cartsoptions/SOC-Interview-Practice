# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-11-02

**Curriculum:** Questions 177–180 | Endpoint DLP and removable media, Linux systemd persistence with Wazuh FIM, Microsoft Sentinel service-principal hunting, and compromised workload-identity response

## Question 177

### Interview Question

Microsoft Purview Endpoint DLP alerts that a departing employee attempted to copy files labelled Confidential to a USB drive. The alert action says `Block with override`, and the user entered “working from home” as the justification. What does DLP do in this situation, and how would you decide whether this is a policy exception, an accidental action, or attempted data exfiltration?

### Interview-Ready Answer

Data loss prevention applies policy to sensitive information and records or restricts risky handling. In this scenario, Endpoint DLP is the control closest to the file action. Microsoft documents that endpoint policies can audit, block, or block with override when a user attempts to copy a protected item to removable media. A recorded override proves that the user proceeded through the permitted override workflow; it does not prove that the business purpose was valid or that the copy completed successfully.

In a SOC environment, I would first validate the event rather than equating “DLP alert” with “insider threat.” I would identify the user, device, file names or classifications, policy and rule, action, enforcement result, override justification, time, USB-device details, and whether the endpoint was online and reporting normally. I would use Purview Activity explorer or the DLP alert dashboard for the policy event and correlate Defender device-control or endpoint telemetry where available. Microsoft Defender for Endpoint can record `RemovableStoragePolicyTriggered` events when an applicable device-control policy has an audit entry, but that evidence is configuration-dependent and distinct from the DLP policy match.

I would then establish context: the employee's role, notice period, approved remote-working method, data ownership, volume and sensitivity, whether the USB is corporate-approved, whether the same files were emailed, uploaded or printed, and whether the manager or data owner authorised the transfer. I would preserve the evidence and involve the data-protection, insider-risk, HR or legal function only through the organisation's approved process; a Tier 1 analyst should not make an employment or intent determination.

If the action was blocked, I would describe it as a prevented or attempted transfer, not completed exfiltration. If telemetry shows a successful copy to an unauthorised device and no legitimate approval, I would escalate as a suspected data-loss incident. Evidence collection of matched files is optional and permission-sensitive; I would access content only when authorised and necessary.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The employee copied 420 customer records to a personally owned USB device after submitting notice. The override was accepted by policy, the manager denies authorising portable-media use, and Activity explorer plus endpoint evidence shows the file-creation activity on the removable drive. No external upload is observed.

- **Observation:** Endpoint DLP matched confidential files during a USB-copy action, and the user supplied an override justification.
- **Hypothesis:** The user may be attempting to remove customer data, but an approved business transfer or misunderstanding remains possible until context is checked.
- **Confirmed evidence:** The files were written to a personally owned removable device, the data owner confirms that the transfer was unauthorised, and the action conflicts with the user's approved work method. Intent and any later disclosure to a third party remain unconfirmed.

The verdict is **high-severity true positive — confirmed unauthorised transfer of sensitive data to removable media, requiring data-loss and insider-risk escalation; malicious intent and onward disclosure are not yet proven**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Confirm the DLP alert, policy, rule, sensitive-information match, action, enforcement result and override details.
2. Identify the user, managed device, timestamps, affected files, classifications, volume and business owner.
3. Determine whether the removable device is approved and capture its available identifiers without seizing or examining personal property outside procedure.
4. Correlate Purview Activity explorer, DLP alerts, Defender device-control events and endpoint file activity where licensed and configured.
5. Check whether the action was attempted, blocked, overridden or completed; keep these states distinct.
6. Review approved remote-working, data-export and removable-media exceptions with the manager or data owner through trusted channels.
7. Scope related email, cloud-upload, print, network-share or other removable-media activity according to the playbook.
8. Preserve evidence and escalate to the authorised data-protection, insider-risk, HR/legal and incident-response owners without asserting motive.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Purview Endpoint DLP alert dashboard and Activity explorer, Microsoft Defender XDR/Defender for Endpoint `DeviceEvents` where configured, device-control telemetry, file-classification and sensitivity-label information, endpoint file activity, approved USB inventory, identity logs, cloud/email audit records and the case-management system.

### Key Interview Takeaway

DLP is a policy-enforcement and evidence source, not an intent detector. I would prove what action occurred, whether it succeeded, what data and device were involved, and whether it was authorised before escalating the confirmed facts.

## Question 178

### Interview Question

Wazuh File Integrity Monitoring alerts that `/etc/systemd/system/update-check.service` was created on a Linux server. Its `ExecStart` points to a script under `/tmp`, and the service begins making outbound connections. How would you determine whether this is an approved service or attacker persistence?

### Interview-Ready Answer

The Wazuh alert proves that a monitored file was created or changed; it does not by itself prove malicious persistence. Wazuh FIM can report the path, before/after hashes or attributes, and—when who-data is correctly configured—the login user, effective user and process associated with the change. If `report_changes` is enabled for a text file, a content diff may also be available. Those capabilities depend on the agent configuration, platform support and telemetry health, so I would verify the raw alert rather than assume every field is present.

Systemd loads system unit files from documented locations including `/etc/systemd/system`. A service unit's `ExecStart` directive defines what starts with the service. MITRE ATT&CK T1543.002 covers adversaries creating or modifying systemd services for persistence. That mapping labels the behaviour; it does not determine intent.

I would inspect the complete unit file, its ownership, permissions, timestamps, symlinks and `ExecStart` target. I would hash and identify the referenced script without executing it, review its signer or package provenance where applicable, and check package-manager, deployment, change-management and administrator records. I would correlate who-data or Linux Audit evidence with authentication, sudo, shell history where policy permits, process creation, service-manager journal records and network connections.

The strongest evidence would be a tight sequence: an unauthorised login or sudo session creates the unit, the unit launches a script from a temporary or user-writable path, and the resulting process communicates externally or performs other unauthorised actions. Within Tier 1 authority, I would preserve the unit and process evidence, isolate or request isolation if active compromise is supported, restrict confirmed malicious egress, and escalate. I would not delete the unit or script during initial triage unless the playbook explicitly authorises that action.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Wazuh who-data attributes the unit-file creation to a `sudo` process in a session opened by the `backup` account from an unfamiliar source. The service starts `/tmp/.cache/update.sh`; EDR or audit telemetry shows the script launching a miner and contacting an external pool. The server owner confirms there was no deployment.

- **Observation:** A new systemd unit points to a script in `/tmp` and is followed by outbound activity.
- **Hypothesis:** A compromised account created a persistent systemd service to relaunch malicious code.
- **Confirmed evidence:** Correlated login, sudo, FIM who-data, unit content, process execution, network telemetry and owner denial establish unauthorised persistence and execution.

The verdict is **critical true positive — confirmed Linux compromise with systemd-service persistence and malicious execution; the original credential-acquisition method remains unconfirmed**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve the full Wazuh alert and confirm FIM mode, monitored path, agent health and timestamps.
2. Review the unit file, `ExecStart` and related directives, ownership, permissions, hashes, symlinks and change diff where safely available.
3. Validate who-data fields such as login user, effective user and modifying process only if the required Audit/eBPF configuration was active.
4. Identify the referenced script or binary without executing it; check its hashes, provenance and presence on other hosts.
5. Correlate authentication, sudo, auditd, process, systemd journal and network evidence.
6. Check package-manager, configuration-management, administrator and approved-change records.
7. Hunt for the unit name, file path, hash, account, source address and external destination across the estate.
8. Invoke authorised host, account and network containment; escalate the timeline, scope and evidence gaps to Tier 2/IR.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Wazuh FIM and who-data alerts, Linux Audit/auditd, systemd journal, authentication and sudo logs, Linux EDR or process telemetry, firewall/DNS/proxy logs, package-manager and configuration-management records, Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk and the asset/change inventory.

### Key Interview Takeaway

A FIM alert proves a monitored change, not malicious intent. I would connect the unit-file change to the responsible session, the executed payload and its impact before confirming systemd persistence.

## Question 179

### Interview Question

An application owner gives you the Microsoft Entra service-principal object ID for a suspected automation identity. Write a safe Microsoft Sentinel KQL query to review its sign-ins during the last 24 hours, and explain what the results can and cannot prove.

### Interview-Ready Answer

Assuming the Microsoft Entra service-principal sign-in log is connected to the workspace and populating `AADServicePrincipalSignInLogs`, I would start with a bounded query:

```kusto
let lookback = 24h;
let targetServicePrincipalId = "00000000-0000-0000-0000-000000000000";
AADServicePrincipalSignInLogs
| where TimeGenerated >= ago(lookback)
| where ServicePrincipalId == targetServicePrincipalId
| project TimeGenerated,
          ServicePrincipalName,
          AppId,
          ServicePrincipalId,
          IPAddress,
          Location,
          ResourceDisplayName,
          ClientCredentialType,
          ServicePrincipalCredentialKeyId,
          ResultType,
          ResultDescription,
          CorrelationId
| order by TimeGenerated asc
```

The all-zero GUID is a simulation placeholder and must be replaced with the actual service-principal object ID. Microsoft documents the table and every projected field. In this table, `ResultType` is described as `Success` or `Failure`. I would still inspect the tenant's current schema and raw records because connectors, transformations, retention and licensing affect what is available.

The query can establish that Sentinel contains sign-in records attributed to the specified service principal, including source address, resource and credential context. It cannot prove that the sign-in was malicious, that the source address represents the actual operator, or that the credential was newly stolen. No results do not prove inactivity; they may reflect the wrong object ID, a short time window, ingestion delay, disabled diagnostic settings, retention limits or use of a different identity type.

I would compare the source addresses, resources, credential key IDs, credential types, frequency and timing with the owner's expected automation pattern. I would pivot to `AuditLogs` for credential or permission changes, workload-identity risk where licensed, resource logs and Microsoft Graph activity where collected. I would not use a geolocation difference alone as a verdict because cloud automation can legitimately run from provider infrastructure or changing egress addresses.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The query shows successful sign-ins using a previously unseen credential key ID from an IP range not used by the automation platform. The service principal accesses Microsoft Graph and a storage resource outside its documented function. Audit logs show an unexpected certificate/secret-management change shortly before the first sign-in.

- **Observation:** The service principal signs in from new infrastructure with a new credential key ID and accesses unusual resources.
- **Hypothesis:** An unauthorised credential was added and used to impersonate the workload identity.
- **Confirmed evidence:** The owner denies the change, audit evidence identifies an unauthorised credential addition, and subsequent sign-in/resource logs show that credential being used outside the approved workload pattern.

The verdict is **high-severity true positive — confirmed service-principal credential compromise and unauthorised resource access; the method used to compromise the credential-administration identity remains under investigation**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Confirm the object ID, time range, connector health, diagnostic settings, ingestion delay and retention.
2. Review raw service-principal sign-ins for result, IP, resource, credential type, credential key ID and correlation ID.
3. Compare the activity with expected hosting platforms, schedules, resources and owner-approved credential inventory.
4. Inspect Entra `AuditLogs` for application/service-principal credential, role and permission changes.
5. Review workload-identity risk detections, Microsoft Graph activity and target-resource logs where licensed and collected.
6. Contact the application owner through an approved channel and validate maintenance or credential rotation.
7. Scope every use of the suspicious key ID, source, app ID and affected resource.
8. Escalate or invoke approved workload-identity containment without deleting evidence or breaking critical automation unexpectedly.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Sentinel, `AADServicePrincipalSignInLogs`, Entra `AuditLogs`, risky workload-identity reports where licensed, Microsoft Graph activity logs where collected, target-resource audit logs, Defender XDR, cloud network telemetry, application inventory and the incident-management system.

### Key Interview Takeaway

KQL retrieves service-principal sign-in evidence; it does not assign intent. I would compare the credential, source and resource pattern with the approved workload and correlate the sign-ins with audit changes and downstream activity.

## Question 180

### Interview Question

Microsoft Entra reports that an administrator added a new credential to a production service principal. Minutes later the workload identity signs in from unfamiliar infrastructure and uses Microsoft Graph and Azure storage outside its normal pattern. How would you contain, scope and escalate this incident as Tier 1 without unnecessarily breaking a critical application?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat this as a critical suspected workload-identity compromise because a credential change is immediately followed by anomalous use. A service principal is a non-human identity used by an application or automation. It can authenticate with a secret, certificate or federated credential and may hold permissions that are different from the administrator's own interactive permissions.

My first task is to preserve a UTC-normalised evidence chain: who or what added the credential, the application and service-principal object IDs, key ID or thumbprint, change time, source address, sign-in records, resources accessed, permissions and downstream actions. Microsoft recommends checking application-management audit logs for unexpected certificate or secret changes and reviewing service-principal sign-ins and target resources. MITRE ATT&CK T1098.001 describes adding cloud credentials to applications or service principals as a persistence or privilege-escalation technique; the mapping does not establish attribution.

Containment must balance security and business impact. I would immediately escalate to the identity, cloud, application-owner and incident-response teams and invoke the approved workload-identity playbook. If authorised, the response may disable service-principal sign-in or remove the confirmed unauthorised credential. Microsoft cautions that disabling an application can cause business impact, so a critical application may require a coordinated eviction: prepare a known-good replacement credential, update dependent services securely, remove compromised credentials, rotate secrets the identity could access, revoke unnecessary permissions, and monitor reactivation. I would not simply reset the administrator's password and assume the service-principal credential is invalidated.

Scoping includes every sign-in and resource touched by the suspicious credential; credential, role, permission and ownership changes; the administrator's sign-ins and sessions; other applications modified by the same actor; and data access or modification in Graph, storage, Key Vault and other target services. A successful sign-in confirms authentication, not data theft. Exfiltration requires resource-level evidence showing that organisational data was retrieved or transferred.

Recovery should use a verified credential inventory, least-privilege permissions, known-good application configuration and monitored restoration. NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 supports integrating detection, response, recovery and improvement into risk management; I would document containment decisions, business impact, remaining exposure and improvement actions.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The administrator denies adding the secret. Audit logs show the change originated from an unfamiliar IP during a stolen session. The new key ID appears in successful service-principal sign-ins that read mail through Microsoft Graph and download files from a restricted storage account. The application owner identifies a safe maintenance window and prepares a replacement certificate.

- **Observation:** A new credential is followed by anomalous service-principal sign-ins and access to resources outside the approved workload.
- **Hypothesis:** An attacker compromised an administrator session, added a credential for persistence and used the service principal's permissions.
- **Confirmed evidence:** Administrator denial, unauthorised credential-change audit records, matching key-ID sign-ins and resource-level read/download records establish compromise and data access. Whether data left the provider environment or was disclosed further requires separate evidence.

The verdict is **critical true positive — confirmed administrator-session and production workload-identity compromise with unauthorised cloud-data access, requiring coordinated identity and application containment**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve Entra audit and service-principal sign-in records, object IDs, key IDs/thumbprints, correlation IDs, permissions and UTC timestamps.
2. Validate the credential addition, initiating user or application, source address, administrator session and approved-change records.
3. Identify all successful and failed uses of the new credential and every target resource accessed.
4. Review new app-role assignments, delegated grants, owners, secrets, certificates and federated credentials.
5. Scope the initiating administrator for suspicious sign-ins, session/token activity and other directory changes.
6. Review Graph, storage, Key Vault and other resource logs before claiming data theft or secret exposure.
7. Coordinate authorised disablement or credential removal with the application owner; protect availability and avoid deleting evidence.
8. Rotate exposed credentials and dependent secrets, revoke unnecessary permissions and restore the workload with known-good credentials under specialist ownership.
9. Hunt for the same actor, source, key ID, app ID, permission changes and access patterns across the tenant.
10. Escalate the evidence, containment choice, business impact, scope and uncertainty; record lessons for credential governance and detections.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Entra audit and service-principal sign-in logs, Microsoft Sentinel, workload-identity risk reports where licensed, Microsoft Graph activity logs, Azure resource and storage audit logs, Key Vault logs, Defender XDR, cloud network telemetry, application/credential inventory, change records and incident management.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Treat a service principal like a powerful non-human identity. Correlate the credential change with its subsequent use, contain the specific access path in coordination with the application owner, and prove downstream impact from resource logs rather than inference.
