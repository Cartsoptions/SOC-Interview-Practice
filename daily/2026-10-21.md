# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-10-21

**Curriculum:** Questions 145–148 | DMARC limits, reply-chain BEC, Defender XDR email hunting, and compromised-mailbox incident response

## Question 145

### Interview Question

A finance user reports an urgent invoice email that appears to come from a known supplier. The message shows SPF, DKIM and DMARC as passing, but it asks the user to pay a new bank account. Would you close it as legitimate? Explain how you would triage it.

### Interview-Ready Answer

No. Passing email authentication is useful evidence about domains and message handling, but it does not prove that the person sending the message is trustworthy or that the business request is genuine.

SPF authorises sending infrastructure for the envelope sender. DKIM verifies that a signing domain took responsibility for a cryptographic signature and that signed content was not altered after signing. Under the current DMARC standard, RFC 9989, a DMARC pass requires a passing SPF or DKIM result whose authenticated domain aligns with the visible Author Domain in the `From` field. That helps detect unauthorised use of a domain, but a criminal can still use a lookalike domain with correctly configured authentication, compromise a legitimate mailbox, or send a malicious request from infrastructure that the domain owner authorised.

In a SOC environment, I would use authentication results as one layer of evidence. I would compare the visible `From`, envelope sender/Return-Path, DKIM signing domain, `Reply-To`, display name and domain spelling. I would review the message path, first-contact or unusual-sender context, URLs, attachments, prior correspondence and delivery verdict. Most importantly, finance should verify the bank-change request through a trusted channel using established supplier contact details, not a phone number or link supplied in the message.

As Tier 1, I would preserve the message and headers, search for other recipients, record the `NetworkMessageId` in Microsoft 365, and escalate or request containment under the email-response playbook. I would not approve payment, contact the attacker, delete evidence, or change mail controls without authority. Email administrators or authorised SecOps staff normally perform tenant-wide remediation and policy changes.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The email uses `accounts@vendor-payments.example`, while the known supplier uses `vendor.example`. SPF, DKIM and DMARC pass for the lookalike domain. The `Reply-To` points to a different free-mail account, and the supplier confirms through its established telephone number that it did not request a bank change.

- **Observation:** Authentication passes for the lookalike domain, but the sender domain, reply path and payment instruction differ from the approved supplier pattern.
- **Hypothesis:** An attacker registered and authenticated a deceptive domain to impersonate the supplier and redirect payment.
- **Confirmed evidence:** Header/domain evidence confirms the message was authenticated for the lookalike domain; out-of-band supplier verification confirms the bank-change request is unauthorised. Authentication does not authenticate the business intent.

The verdict is **true positive — supplier-impersonation BEC/phishing using an authenticated lookalike domain**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve the original message, full headers, attachments, URLs, recipient list, arrival time and alert/case identifiers.
2. Compare the visible `From`, `SenderMailFromAddress`/Return-Path, DKIM signing domain, `Reply-To`, display name and exact domain spelling.
3. Read the SPF, DKIM, DMARC and composite-authentication results without treating a pass as a benign verdict.
4. Check domain age and reputation only as enrichment; newly registered or low-reputation infrastructure raises suspicion but is not proof by itself.
5. Review prior legitimate supplier messages, whether this is first contact, normal invoice format, usual payment workflow and any urgent or secrecy language.
6. Inspect URLs and attachments with approved tooling; do not open or detonate them on a production endpoint.
7. Search by `NetworkMessageId`, sender, subject, domain, URL and attachment hash to identify other recipients and interactions.
8. Ask finance/procurement to validate the request through the approved supplier record and place any payment on hold under procedure.
9. Escalate with the facts, scope, verdict and requested actions; request purge/blocking only through authorised workflows.

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365 Threat Explorer/Real-time detections, message trace and email entity data; Proofpoint or another secure email gateway where deployed; full message headers and `Authentication-Results`; DNS/WHOIS and reputation enrichment; proxy/DNS logs; Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk; finance/vendor records and case management.

### Interview Takeaway

A DMARC pass means an aligned SPF or DKIM path authenticated the visible domain; it does not prove the sender's identity or the legitimacy of a payment request. I would combine header evidence, content/context, tenant-wide scoping and independent business verification.

## Question 146

### Interview Question

A supplier's genuine Microsoft 365 mailbox replies inside an existing invoice thread and provides new bank details. SPF, DKIM and DMARC pass, the domain is correct, and the writing style looks familiar. How would you investigate possible reply-chain BEC?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat this as potentially high impact because a compromised real mailbox can inherit the legitimate domain, thread history and normal authentication results. The right question is no longer only “was the domain spoofed?” but “was the supplier's account or conversation taken over, and is the financial instruction authorised?”

I would preserve the reported message and correlate its `NetworkMessageId`, Internet Message ID, sender, recipients, subject and timing. I would compare it with earlier messages in the thread: were recipients removed or added, did the reply address change, was an old attachment replaced, and did the payment process suddenly deviate? I would search our environment for the same sender, subject, URL, attachment or bank detail and determine whether anyone clicked, replied, disclosed data or initiated a payment.

Secure email products can add useful behavioural signals. Proofpoint describes BEC detection that evaluates impersonation, supplier fraud and compromised accounts; Abnormal describes behavioural baselining for unusual identity and account activity. Those product verdicts are enrichment, not proof. I would verify the actual evidence available in the deployed product and avoid claiming a feature or automatic action that the tenant does not have.

Because the suspected mailbox belongs to an external supplier, our SOC usually cannot inspect or contain that account directly. I would have finance or vendor management call the supplier using an independently held number, ask the supplier's security team to investigate its mailbox and identity logs, and preserve a clear chain of communication. If the affected mailbox is internal, I would also review Entra sign-ins, audit activity, Sent/Deleted Items, inbox/forwarding rules, authentication-method changes, OAuth consent and device telemetry.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The supplier confirms by telephone that it did not change its bank account. Its security team later confirms an attacker signed into the mailbox and added a rule that hid replies about invoices. Three employees received the fraudulent bank details; one payment was prepared but not released.

- **Observation:** The message is from the supplier's genuine authenticated account within a real thread, and the request changes a sensitive payment control.
- **Hypothesis:** The supplier mailbox was compromised and used to hijack the invoice conversation.
- **Confirmed evidence:** Independent supplier confirmation establishes that the payment instruction is unauthorised; the supplier's sign-in and inbox-rule findings confirm account compromise. Until that evidence arrives, the SOC should label account takeover as a hypothesis, not a fact.

The verdict is **confirmed reply-chain BEC from a compromised supplier mailbox; internal payment fraud was attempted but not completed**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve the full message, headers, thread, attachments, URLs and financial instruction; record who reported it and when.
2. Identify all internal recipients and search for related messages by message identifiers, sender, subject, domain, attachment/hash, URL and bank details where policy permits.
3. Compare the message with known-good correspondence: recipients, reply address, timing, language, signature, attachment format and payment workflow.
4. Establish user actions: opened only, clicked, replied, supplied information, entered credentials, downloaded a file or initiated/authorised payment.
5. Validate the request with the supplier using approved contact data and involve finance/vendor management immediately.
6. Review secure-email gateway and Defender verdicts as supporting context, and document what the product actually observed.
7. If an internal identity is implicated, review Entra sign-ins, audit logs, mailbox rules/forwarding, Sent/Deleted Items, OAuth consents, authentication-method changes and endpoint telemetry.
8. Request approved message remediation and indicator blocking, while recognising that static indicators may change and a legitimate supplier domain cannot simply be blocked without business impact review.
9. Escalate to the fraud/IR path with payment status, recipient scope, evidence, user actions and supplier confirmation.

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365, Threat Explorer/Real-time detections, Exchange message trace and audit data; Proofpoint or Abnormal Security where deployed; Entra sign-in and audit logs for internal identities; Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk; endpoint/proxy/DNS telemetry; supplier master-data and payment-approval records; case management.

### Interview Takeaway

Reply-chain BEC can pass authentication because the attacker is using a real account. I would verify the transaction out of band, scope recipients and actions, separate hypotheses from confirmed evidence, and coordinate with finance and the supplier's security team.

## Question 147

### Interview Question

You are given the Microsoft 365 `NetworkMessageId` of the suspicious supplier email. Show a safe Microsoft Defender XDR Advanced Hunting query to retrieve the email and its URLs, explain the result, and state what it cannot prove.

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would run the following in Microsoft Defender XDR Advanced Hunting, replacing the placeholder with the actual Microsoft 365 network message ID:

```kusto
let suspiciousMessage = "<network-message-id>";
EmailEvents
| where NetworkMessageId == suspiciousMessage
| project Timestamp,
          NetworkMessageId,
          InternetMessageId,
          SenderDisplayName,
          SenderFromAddress,
          SenderMailFromAddress,
          RecipientEmailAddress,
          Subject,
          DeliveryAction,
          DeliveryLocation,
          LatestDeliveryAction,
          LatestDeliveryLocation,
          AuthenticationDetails,
          ThreatTypes,
          DetectionMethods,
          AttachmentCount,
          UrlCount
| join kind=leftouter (
    EmailUrlInfo
    | where NetworkMessageId == suspiciousMessage
    | project NetworkMessageId, Url, UrlDomain, UrlLocation
) on NetworkMessageId
| order by Timestamp asc
```

Microsoft documents `EmailEvents` as Defender for Office 365 email-processing telemetry and `EmailUrlInfo` as URL data extracted from emails and attachments. `NetworkMessageId` is the common Microsoft 365 identifier used here for the join. The projected fields are documented schema fields; `AuthenticationDetails` contains pass/fail results for mechanisms such as SPF, DKIM, DMARC and composite authentication. `UrlLocation` shows where the URL was found and can identify a URL extracted from a QR code.

The query helps answer who received the message, which visible and envelope sender addresses were recorded, the delivery state, authentication details, security verdicts and which URLs were associated with the message. A left outer join keeps the email row even if no URL record exists.

The output does not prove that a URL is malicious, that a user clicked it, that credentials were entered, that an account was compromised or that payment fraud occurred. It also depends on Defender for Office 365 being deployed and on the tenant's data availability. `LatestDeliveryAction` and `LatestDeliveryLocation` are documented for advanced hunting but are not available in the Streaming API. Before operational use, I would confirm the live schema in the portal, replace the placeholder, and interpret blank fields as missing telemetry rather than proof that an action did not happen.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The query returns five recipients. The email was delivered to four inboxes and one junk folder. Authentication passed, two invoice URLs were extracted, and the current threat fields are blank. Proxy telemetry later confirms that one recipient visited a URL, but there is no evidence yet of credential submission.

- **Observation:** The message reached five recipients; authentication passed; the message contains two URLs; separate proxy telemetry shows one visit.
- **Hypothesis:** The visited page may have attempted credential theft or payment redirection.
- **Confirmed evidence:** Email telemetry confirms delivery and associated URLs; proxy logs confirm a visit. Credential theft or account compromise remains unconfirmed until identity, endpoint or user evidence supports it.

The initial verdict is **suspicious BEC/phishing email with one confirmed URL visit; compromise impact remains under investigation**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Validate the `NetworkMessageId`, time range, recipients and whether multiple records represent different recipients or delivery updates.
2. Compare `SenderFromAddress` with `SenderMailFromAddress`, display name, authentication details and known supplier addresses.
3. Review `DeliveryAction`, `DeliveryLocation`, latest delivery fields, threat verdicts and detection methods without treating blank verdicts as safe.
4. Inspect `Url`, `UrlDomain` and `UrlLocation` using approved analysis; note QR-code extraction when applicable.
5. Pivot to attachment information if `AttachmentCount` is non-zero and retrieve hashes from the appropriate documented table or email entity.
6. Search proxy/DNS, endpoint and identity telemetry for visits, downloads, process execution, sign-ins, MFA/authentication changes and session anomalies.
7. Contact recipients to establish actions and preserve their statements; reset or contain only according to evidence and playbook authority.
8. Request authorised message remediation across all affected mailboxes and validate the resulting action status.
9. Document query time, scope, data gaps and the distinction between message evidence, user interaction and compromise evidence.

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender XDR Advanced Hunting; `EmailEvents` and `EmailUrlInfo`; Defender for Office 365 Threat Explorer/Real-time detections and email entity; proxy/DNS and Defender for Endpoint telemetry; Entra sign-in/audit logs; Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk; case management.

### Interview Takeaway

I would use a documented identifier and documented schema fields to scope the message and its URLs, then corroborate user activity and compromise in other telemetry. A delivered email or URL record is evidence of exposure, not proof of interaction or account takeover.

## Question 148

### Interview Question

Defender alerts that the CFO's Microsoft 365 mailbox sent payment-change emails to staff and external suppliers. The messages are genuinely from the CFO's account, an unknown inbox-forwarding rule exists, and finance has placed a transfer on hold. How would you triage, contain, escalate and support recovery as Tier 1?

### Interview-Ready Answer

This is a high-severity identity and BEC incident. Genuine sent messages plus an unauthorised forwarding rule are strong evidence that the mailbox or an authorised session was abused. I would treat the account compromise as confirmed unless an approved business change explains the activity, while keeping the initial access method, data exposure and payment outcome as separate questions.

I would start a time-normalised timeline covering the alert, sign-ins, token/session activity, mailbox/audit events, rule creation, message sends, recipient actions and finance decisions. I would preserve the incident IDs, message identifiers, rule details, sign-in records and user statement. I would scope internal and external recipients and search for replies, deleted messages, further forwarding, OAuth consent, new authentication methods, role or group changes and other affected identities or devices.

Microsoft's current compromised-identity guidance recommends revoking active sessions and refresh tokens, forcing a password reset or secret rotation, considering temporary disablement when risk remains and policy permits, preserving evidence, and removing malicious inbox/forwarding rules, OAuth consent or unauthorised authentication methods. It also calls for endpoint scanning or reimaging if endpoint compromise is suspected, plus review of role assignments, group memberships and application permissions. I would invoke or request those actions through the approved playbook. If Tier 1 lacks permission, I would urgently escalate to the identity/IR owner rather than improvising privileged changes.

The finance response runs in parallel: keep the transfer on hold, verify payee instructions through trusted contacts, notify fraud/banking and legal/compliance functions according to the incident plan, and record whether funds left the organisation. I would request authorised email remediation for fraudulent messages and warn affected recipients using a trusted channel. I would avoid mass-blocking the CFO or a legitimate supplier domain without assessing business impact.

If the forwarding rule was used to collect email, MITRE ATT&CK T1114.003, Email Forwarding Rule, is a defensible behaviour mapping. It describes the observed technique; it does not identify the attacker or prove how the session was obtained. Recovery requires removing persistence, returning the identity and device to a trusted state, confirming mailbox control, validating that fraudulent messages and rules are addressed, monitoring for recurrence and capturing lessons learned.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Entra logs show a successful sign-in inconsistent with the CFO's normal pattern shortly before the forwarding rule was created. The CFO denies both events. The fraudulent message reached 22 recipients; two replied, one transfer was prepared but stopped, and no endpoint malware is yet confirmed.

- **Observation:** The account sent unauthorised payment messages; an unknown forwarding rule exists; an anomalous successful sign-in precedes the activity.
- **Hypothesis:** An attacker obtained credentials or a valid session/token, established mailbox persistence and attempted payment fraud. Phishing, adversary-in-the-middle activity, token theft and endpoint compromise are possible initial-access explanations.
- **Confirmed evidence:** The CFO's denial, mailbox activity and rule creation confirm unauthorised account use and BEC. The transfer was stopped, so attempted fraud is confirmed but financial loss is not. The precise access method and any data theft remain unconfirmed pending investigation.

The verdict is **true positive — confirmed Microsoft 365 account compromise and BEC with attempted payment fraud; initial access and data-exposure scope remain under investigation**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Confirm the identity, executive/business criticality, alert sources, timestamps/time zones and current payment status.
2. Preserve sign-in and audit records, incident/alert IDs, message identifiers, inbox/forwarding rule details, consent/authentication-method changes and the CFO's statement.
3. Review successful and failed sign-ins, IP/location, client/application, authentication requirement, Conditional Access result, device and session/token context; do not assume MFA success rules out stolen-token abuse.
4. Review Sent/Deleted Items, mailbox audit activity, forwarding/inbox rules, delegate access, OAuth consent, new authentication methods, role/group changes and external forwarding.
5. Scope fraudulent messages, recipients, replies, clicks, credential entry, downloads, payments and data disclosure; warn and support affected parties through trusted channels.
6. Invoke or request approved containment: revoke sessions/tokens, reset credentials, temporarily disable the account if authorised and necessary, remove malicious persistence, remediate messages and protect related accounts.
7. Check the CFO's devices with EDR and escalate for isolation, scan or rebuild when endpoint compromise is suspected and the playbook authorises it.
8. Coordinate finance/fraud actions, including payment hold/recall and bank contact, and involve legal/privacy/communications under the incident plan where impact warrants it.
9. Escalate with confirmed facts, hypotheses, gaps, timeline, scope, actions, evidence locations and decisions required.
10. Support recovery by validating identity controls, mailbox rules and permissions, message remediation, endpoint health and heightened monitoring; record lessons for detection and process improvements.

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender XDR incident and Advanced Hunting, Defender for Office 365, Entra sign-in and audit logs, Exchange mailbox/audit and forwarding-rule evidence, Defender for Endpoint, Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk, Proofpoint/Abnormal Security where deployed, finance/payment records, identity administration and case management.

### Interview Takeaway

For confirmed mailbox compromise, I would separate what is proven from the unknown entry method, contain the identity and fraud risk in parallel, remove persistence, scope messages and recipient actions, escalate within authority, and validate a trusted recovery.

## Verification Notes

- RFC 9989, published in May 2026, is the current DMARC Proposed Standard and obsoletes RFCs 7489 and 9091. It states that DMARC pass requires an SPF or DKIM pass whose authenticated domain aligns with the RFC5322.From/Author Domain. The scenarios correctly avoid treating that pass as proof of human identity or benign intent.
- RFC 8601 defines the `Authentication-Results` header field used to communicate message-authentication results. Header interpretation remains contextual because intermediaries and trust boundaries matter.
- Microsoft documents the `EmailEvents` fields used in Question 147, including `NetworkMessageId`, visible and envelope sender addresses, delivery fields and `AuthenticationDetails`. Microsoft documents `EmailUrlInfo` fields `NetworkMessageId`, `Url`, `UrlDomain` and `UrlLocation`; `UrlLocation` can identify QR-code-extracted URLs.
- The query uses a documented join key and fields. It does not invent an `ActionType` or claim click/credential telemetry from email tables. `LatestDeliveryAction` and `LatestDeliveryLocation` are unavailable in the Streaming API but documented in Advanced Hunting.
- Microsoft states that the two advanced-hunting tables are populated by Defender for Office 365; without that service they may not work or return data. Tenant licensing, retention and telemetry coverage must be checked.
- Microsoft documents message investigation and remediation through Threat Explorer/Real-time detections and Action Center. Specific remediation actions and approvals depend on role, licensing and tenant policy, so the answers use “invoke or request authorised action.”
- Microsoft's compromised-account and compromised-identity guidance supports reviewing mailbox rules, forwarding, Sent/Deleted Items, MFA methods and application consent; revoking sessions/tokens; resetting credentials; removing persistence; reviewing permissions; and checking endpoints when compromise is suspected.
- MITRE ATT&CK T1114.003 covers adversaries using email-forwarding rules to collect email. The mapping is used only when a malicious forwarding rule is evidenced; it does not establish attribution or initial access.
- Proofpoint and Abnormal Security vendor material is used only to support the general availability of behavioural/identity-based BEC detection in their products. A vendor alert is treated as enrichment requiring evidence review, and no unsupported automatic response is claimed.
- All artefacts, domains, identities, message IDs and transaction details in the scenarios are simulated.
