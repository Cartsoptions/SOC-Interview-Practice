# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-11-17

**Curriculum:** Questions 221–224 | Risk-based vulnerability prioritisation, EDR malware triage, Splunk/Sysmon execution-chain analysis, and ransomware incident response

## Question 221

### Interview Question

A vulnerability scanner reports three findings: a CVSS 9.8 vulnerability on an isolated development server with no known exploitation; a CVSS 8.1 vulnerability on an internet-facing remote-access gateway that appears in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog; and a CVSS 7.5 vulnerability on a public web server protected by a WAF. Which would you prioritise, and how would you handle the finding as a Tier 1 SOC analyst?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would not rank the findings by CVSS alone. CVSS communicates technical severity, but NIST explicitly states that it is not a complete measure of risk. I would prioritise the internet-facing remote-access gateway first because the vulnerability is known to be exploited, the asset is externally reachable, and compromise could provide an initial-access path into the organisation.

My first step would be to validate that the scanner has identified the correct product, version and asset. I would confirm the gateway's owner, external exposure, business criticality, patch status and whether the vendor advisory says the installed version is affected. Version-only detection can be misleading where a vendor has backported a fix or where scanner credentials and evidence are incomplete.

I would then combine several inputs rather than treat any one score as a verdict:

- **CVSS:** technical severity, not organisation-specific risk.
- **CISA KEV:** reliable evidence that the vulnerability has been exploited in the wild and clear remediation guidance.
- **EPSS:** an estimate of the probability that exploitation activity will be observed in the next 30 days; it is not an impact or complete risk score.
- **Local context:** asset exposure, data and service criticality, privilege required, reachable attack path, compensating controls and signs of exploitation.

CISA's vulnerability-management guidance separates the work into identifying assets and vulnerabilities, assessing and prioritising them, acting through remediation, mitigation or accepted risk, and validating the result. For the gateway, I would create or update the vulnerability ticket with the evidence, notify the vulnerability-management and system owners, and escalate the KEV exposure according to the organisation's SLA. If immediate patching is unsafe, authorised owners may apply vendor-recommended mitigations or restrict exposure while a tested patch is prepared.

Tier 1 should not independently patch a production gateway or declare an exception. I would search SIEM, EDR, firewall, VPN and identity telemetry for exploitation indicators, document whether compromise evidence exists, and keep incident response separate from—but connected to—the remediation ticket. After the approved change, I would verify the installed version or configuration, confirm service health and request an authenticated rescan before closure.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The scanner confirms that the internet-facing gateway runs an affected version. It is present in KEV, the vendor has released a fix, and firewall logs show repeated requests matching the affected service path from several hosting-provider addresses. There is no confirmed successful exploitation in EDR or identity logs.

- **Observation:** An externally reachable, business-critical gateway is affected by a vulnerability known to be exploited in the wild.
- **Hypothesis:** Threat actors may be scanning for or attempting to exploit the gateway.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Asset inventory, authenticated scanner evidence, vendor version guidance and KEV status confirm urgent exposure; network requests confirm targeting but do not by themselves prove successful exploitation.

The verdict is **critical remediation priority with suspected exploitation attempts; system compromise remains unconfirmed pending host, authentication and network evidence**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Scanner plugin evidence, authentication status and detection method.
2. Exact product, version, configuration and vendor advisory.
3. CISA KEV status, CVSS vector and current EPSS probability.
4. Internet exposure, attack path, asset owner and business criticality.
5. Existing WAF, ACL, segmentation, EDR and other compensating controls.
6. Firewall, VPN, web, identity and endpoint evidence of attempted or successful exploitation.
7. Patch, mitigation or risk-acceptance owner and approved SLA.
8. Change record, deployment result and service-health checks.
9. Authenticated rescan or equivalent validation after remediation.
10. Incident escalation if exploitation or post-exploitation activity is found.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Tenable, Qualys, Rapid7 or another authorised vulnerability scanner; asset inventory/CMDB; CISA KEV; NVD and vendor advisories; EPSS; Microsoft Defender Vulnerability Management or other exposure platform; Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk; WAF, firewall, VPN, proxy, identity and endpoint logs; change and ticketing systems.

### Key Interview Takeaway

CVSS tells me technical severity, not the whole risk. I prioritise validated exposure by combining active exploitation, reachability, asset impact and controls, then support authorised remediation and prove the result through validation or rescan.

## Question 222

### Interview Question

Microsoft Defender for Endpoint alerts that `winword.exe` launched `powershell.exe` with an encoded command, after which a DLL appeared in a user's temporary directory and `rundll32.exe` executed it. What is EDR providing in this scenario, and how would you triage the alert without assuming that every PowerShell or rundll32 execution is malware?

### Interview-Ready Answer

Endpoint detection and response records endpoint activity, applies behavioral detections and gives analysts investigation and authorised response capabilities. In this case, the value is the execution chain: a document application spawned a script interpreter, the script was associated with a new file, and a trusted Windows utility then loaded or executed the DLL. Each component can be legitimate in isolation; their parent-child relationship, timing, command lines, file origin and follow-on behavior determine the risk.

Microsoft documents `DeviceProcessEvents` fields including `Timestamp`, `DeviceName`, `FileName`, `FolderPath`, `ProcessCommandLine`, `SHA1`, account information, `InitiatingProcessFileName`, `InitiatingProcessCommandLine` and process identifiers. I would use the device timeline and process tree to preserve the exact sequence, then examine the original document, its delivery source, Mark-of-the-Web or download context where available, the PowerShell command, any decoded content in a safe analysis process, the DLL path and hash, signer information, network destinations and subsequent persistence or credential-access activity.

MITRE ATT&CK treats a document or viewer spawning an unusual child such as PowerShell or rundll32 as a useful malicious-file execution pattern, while PowerShell abuse is T1059.001 and rundll32 proxy execution is T1218.011. Those mappings describe behavior; they do not prove malicious intent by themselves.

I would check file and process prevalence across the environment, reputation sources, email and web evidence, device criticality and whether the activity matches approved administration or software deployment. A hash match from a trusted intelligence source strengthens identification, but an unknown or clean reputation result does not clear a newly created file. I would not upload a confidential sample to a public service unless policy explicitly permits it.

If the chain includes an untrusted document, successful payload creation, suspicious external communication or persistence and no approved explanation, I would classify it as a true positive and immediately escalate. Within authorised permissions, response owners may isolate the device, collect an investigation package, run an antivirus scan or use Live Response. Tier 1 should preserve the timeline and avoid killing processes, deleting files or isolating critical devices outside the playbook.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The attachment came from a newly registered external domain. Defender shows Word spawning encoded PowerShell, the creation of an unsigned DLL in the user's temporary directory, rundll32 loading that DLL, a connection to a rare external host and a new scheduled-task persistence event. No approved software deployment or administrator activity exists.

- **Observation:** A document initiated a script and DLL execution chain followed by network and persistence activity.
- **Hypothesis:** The attachment delivered a staged malware payload.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Email provenance, endpoint process and file telemetry, DLL execution, rare external communication and persistence establish malicious execution.

The verdict is **high-severity true-positive malware execution with command-and-control and persistence; wider credential and lateral-movement impact remains under investigation**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Alert source, timestamp, device, user and asset criticality.
2. Complete process tree and exact command lines.
3. Document source, sender, URL, attachment and user action.
4. File path, creation time, hashes, signer and environment prevalence.
5. PowerShell telemetry and decoded content through approved analysis methods.
6. DLL load or execution details and rundll32 command line.
7. DNS, proxy and endpoint network connections.
8. Scheduled tasks, services, registry and other persistence evidence.
9. Credential access, additional hosts and lateral movement indicators.
10. Containment authority, evidence preservation and escalation scope.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Endpoint and Defender XDR device timeline, `DeviceProcessEvents`, `DeviceFileEvents`, `DeviceNetworkEvents` and certificate information where available; Defender for Office 365, Proofpoint or Abnormal Security email evidence; Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk; PowerShell logs; Sysmon; DNS, proxy and firewall logs; VirusTotal or another approved reputation source; incident-management records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

EDR gives me correlated endpoint evidence and response options, not automatic certainty. I validate the complete chain—document, process, file, network and persistence—before declaring malware and escalating authorised containment.

## Question 223

### Interview Question

The same endpoint telemetry is forwarded to Splunk from Sysmon. Write a first-pass SPL search to find Microsoft Office applications spawning suspicious child processes, and explain what the results prove and what still needs validation.

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would begin with a narrow, readable hunt against Sysmon Process Create events. The actual index, sourcetype and extracted field names must be confirmed in the organisation because Splunk add-ons and ingestion pipelines can normalise them differently.

```spl
index=endpoint sourcetype="XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational" EventCode=1
| eval parent_lower=lower(ParentImage), image_lower=lower(Image)
| where match(parent_lower, "\\\\(winword|excel|powerpnt|outlook)\\.exe$")
    AND match(image_lower, "\\\\(powershell|pwsh|cmd|wscript|cscript|mshta|rundll32|regsvr32)\\.exe$")
| table _time host User ParentImage Image ParentCommandLine CommandLine ProcessGuid ParentProcessGuid Hashes
| sort 0 _time
```

Microsoft documents Sysmon Event ID 1 as process creation and states that it can record full parent and child command lines, hashes and a `ProcessGuid` suitable for correlation. Sysmon records observations rather than deciding whether they are malicious. If the environment uses a different field extraction, I would inspect raw events and adapt `ParentImage`, `Image`, `CommandLine`, `ProcessGuid` and related names rather than claim the query is portable without change.

The search proves only that Splunk received Process Create records in which an Office application launched one of the selected child processes. That is a strong hunting lead because the relationship is uncommon in many environments, but legitimate macros, accessibility tools, add-ins, administrators or business automation may produce similar activity.

I would pivot on `ProcessGuid`, device, user and time into Sysmon Event ID 3 network events if that event is enabled, Event ID 11 file creation, PowerShell script-block telemetry, EDR data, DNS/proxy logs and the originating email. Sysmon network connection logging is disabled by default, so the absence of Event ID 3 cannot be treated as proof that no connection occurred. I would also check whether all relevant endpoints are forwarding the channel and whether filtering excluded the activity.

I would turn this hunt into a detection only after baselining legitimate parent-child pairs, documenting exclusions, validating field extractions, mapping device and user entities, testing the time window and ensuring analysts can reach the supporting evidence.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The hunt returns twelve events. Eleven belong to a signed finance add-in approved by the application owner. The remaining event is Word spawning encoded PowerShell on a laptop immediately after an external attachment was opened; the same `ProcessGuid` sequence connects to file creation and rare outbound traffic.

- **Observation:** Twelve Office-to-script child-process events matched the hunt.
- **Hypothesis:** At least one event may represent malicious document execution.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Baseline and ownership evidence clear the finance add-in; email, process, file and network evidence confirm the remaining malware chain.

The verdict is **one high-severity true-positive malware execution and eleven authorised business-automation events suitable for a carefully scoped exclusion**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Correct index, sourcetype, time range and data coverage.
2. Raw Sysmon event and extracted-field accuracy.
3. Parent and child image paths and command lines.
4. `ProcessGuid` and `ParentProcessGuid` correlation.
5. Hashes, signer, prevalence and approved software ownership.
6. Related file creation, network, PowerShell and EDR events.
7. Email, URL, attachment and user-interaction evidence.
8. Similar events across other users and devices.
9. Exclusion scope, expiry/review date and detection ownership.
10. Escalation and containment authority for confirmed cases.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Splunk Enterprise Security or Splunk search; Sysmon Event ID 1 and, where configured, Events 3 and 11; Windows PowerShell Operational logs; Microsoft Defender for Endpoint; Wazuh where it collects Sysmon/Windows telemetry; Microsoft Sentinel; DNS, proxy, firewall and email-security evidence; change and application-inventory records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

SPL can surface a suspicious execution relationship, but the result is a lead rather than a malware verdict. I validate the raw event, correlate the process GUID into file and network activity, and baseline legitimate automation before escalating or tuning.

## Question 224

### Interview Question

Defender alerts on a finance file server after a process deletes volume shadow copies, hundreds of files are rapidly renamed, a ransom note appears and the server opens many SMB sessions to other hosts. How would you prioritise and handle the incident as Tier 1, and how would NIST, MITRE ATT&CK, the Cyber Kill Chain and the Pyramid of Pain help you explain your reasoning?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat this as a critical, active ransomware incident because multiple independent signals show impact and a credible risk of spread. MITRE ATT&CK maps deletion of recovery mechanisms to Inhibit System Recovery, T1490, and encryption of data to Data Encrypted for Impact, T1486. The SMB activity raises the possibility of lateral propagation, but I would validate destination hosts and successful access rather than assume every session spread ransomware.

I would preserve the alert, device timeline, process tree, command lines, file events, ransom note, user and service-account context, network destinations and timestamps. I would immediately escalate through the ransomware playbook and on-call path. If authorised, Defender for Endpoint can isolate a device while retaining connectivity to the Defender service; otherwise I would request isolation from the responsible responder and clearly communicate that the server is business-critical.

CISA's ransomware response guidance prioritises determining affected systems and isolating them in a coordinated manner. Powering a device down can destroy volatile evidence and is recommended only when network disconnection is not possible and the incident team accepts that trade-off. Tier 1 should therefore avoid unapproved shutdowns, deletion or recovery actions.

I would scope other hosts for the same process, hash, command line, account, network destinations, ransom-note name, shadow-copy activity, mass file changes and abnormal SMB authentication. I would ask infrastructure and backup owners whether protected backups and restore points are intact, but I would not initiate restoration until containment, eradication decisions and trusted recovery criteria are established.

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 integrates incident response with CSF 2.0. Govern, Identify and Protect support preparation; Detect, Respond and Recover cover finding, managing, containing, eradicating and restoring; lessons feed continuous improvement. Operationally, I would maintain a timeline, preserve evidence, support containment and scoping, hand off eradication to authorised responders, and validate recovery through clean systems, restored data, monitoring and post-incident improvements.

The frameworks add context rather than replace evidence:

- **MITRE ATT&CK** names the observed adversary behaviors and supports hunting for related techniques.
- **Cyber Kill Chain** shows that the alert is already at actions on objectives; the team must hunt backward for delivery, exploitation, installation and command-and-control evidence while stopping impact.
- **Pyramid of Pain** reminds me that a hash is easy for an attacker to change, while infrastructure, tools and repeatable behaviors such as recovery deletion plus mass encryption are more durable detection opportunities.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

EDR confirms that an unsigned executable launched the recovery-deletion command, wrote the ransom note and modified thousands of files. The same compromised service account authenticated to two other file servers, and one of them begins the same mass-renaming pattern. Backup management shows that offline recovery copies remain intact.

- **Observation:** Recovery deletion, high-volume file changes, ransom-note creation and abnormal SMB activity occurred on a critical server.
- **Hypothesis:** Ransomware is encrypting the server and propagating through a compromised service account.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Endpoint execution and file evidence confirm ransomware impact; authentication and matching behavior on a second server confirm lateral spread.

The verdict is **critical confirmed ransomware incident affecting at least two servers, with ongoing scoping required before eradication and trusted recovery**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Exact detection time, affected server, business service and incident severity.
2. Process tree, executable path, command line, hashes, signer and initiating account.
3. Volume-shadow-copy, backup-catalog and security-control tampering.
4. File-write/rename volume, extensions, ransom-note locations and affected shares.
5. SMB destinations, authentication results and use of privileged or service accounts.
6. Same hashes, tools, infrastructure and behavioral sequence on other hosts.
7. EDR, SIEM, Wazuh, Windows, firewall, DNS, proxy and identity evidence.
8. Approved isolation authority and business-continuity implications.
9. Backup integrity, critical restoration order and clean recovery criteria.
10. Evidence preservation, stakeholder updates, lessons learned and detection improvements.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Endpoint/Defender XDR, Microsoft Sentinel, Splunk, Wazuh, Sysmon and Windows Security logs, SMB and file-server auditing, firewall/DNS/proxy telemetry, Active Directory or Entra identity logs, backup and virtualization platforms, asset inventory, incident-management and out-of-band communication channels.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Multiple impact signals make this a critical incident, not merely a suspicious alert. Tier 1 should preserve evidence, escalate immediately, support authorised isolation and scope the spread, while eradication and recovery proceed only through the incident plan and verified clean-state criteria.
