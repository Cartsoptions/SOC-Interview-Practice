# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-11-24

**Curriculum:** Questions 241–244 | Supplier-thread BEC, Microsoft Defender for Endpoint malware triage, risk-based vulnerability prioritisation, and NIST-aligned ransomware response

## Question 241

### Interview Question

An accounts-payable employee receives a reply inside a genuine supplier email thread asking that an urgent invoice be paid to a new bank account. Microsoft Defender for Office 365 shows that the message was delivered, and the `Authentication-Results` header records SPF, DKIM and DMARC passes. How would you determine whether this is legitimate correspondence, sender spoofing or business email compromise?

### Interview-Ready Answer

SPF, DKIM and DMARC help establish whether a message was authorised and authenticated for the relevant sending domain and alignment rules. They do not prove that the person intended to send the message or that the sending mailbox was safe. A message sent from a compromised supplier account can pass all three checks.

I would preserve the original message and full headers, including the internet `Message-ID`, sender, display name, `From`, `Reply-To`, return path, sending infrastructure, timestamps and `Authentication-Results`. I would compare the requested payment change with the normal supplier process, prior thread language, invoice details and approved bank records. I would never verify the request by replying to the suspicious message or calling a number contained only in it; the supplier should be contacted through a known, independently verified channel.

In Defender for Office 365 and Exchange message trace, I would establish delivery status, all internal recipients, detections, policy actions and whether other similar messages exist. Where licensed and configured, I would review user-reported-message evidence, URL or attachment analysis and available user-interaction evidence. I would also check the internal recipient for suspicious sign-ins, inbox-rule changes, forwarding, unusual sent messages or consent activity, because BEC may involve either the supplier, the organisation or both.

If Proofpoint or Abnormal Security is deployed, I would use its message and identity findings as additional evidence, while validating the verdict against raw headers, message trace, mailbox and identity logs. Exact fields and capabilities depend on the organisation's product, licence and configuration.

Tier 1 can preserve and scope the email, warn affected users, create a high-priority incident and request authorised message removal or account containment. Payment holds, bank changes, mailbox remediation and external notifications must follow financial-fraud and incident-response procedures.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The supplier is contacted through the telephone number in the approved vendor record and confirms that no bank change was requested. The email came from the supplier's genuine domain and authenticated successfully, but the supplier confirms that its mailbox was compromised. Three internal recipients received the message, one opened it, and no payment was released.

- **Observation:** A delivered, authenticated supplier-thread message requests an urgent bank-account change.
- **Hypothesis:** An attacker controlling the supplier mailbox has hijacked a legitimate conversation to redirect payment.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Independent supplier confirmation, an unauthorised payment request from the genuine account and campaign scoping confirm BEC; authentication passes explain why simple spoofing controls did not reject it.

The verdict is **confirmed supplier-account BEC and attempted payment fraud; no internal account compromise or financial loss is confirmed from the available evidence**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Original message, full headers and internet `Message-ID`.
2. `From`, `Reply-To`, return path, display name and sending route.
3. SPF, DKIM, DMARC and composite-authentication results.
4. Message trace, delivery action, recipients and related messages.
5. URLs, attachments and available user-interaction evidence.
6. Thread history, writing style, invoice data and approved bank records.
7. Independent supplier verification through a trusted contact route.
8. Internal sign-ins, mailbox rules, forwarding, sent items and delegate changes.
9. Whether any recipient replied, disclosed data or initiated payment.
10. Authorised message remediation, payment hold, escalation and notification.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365; Exchange message trace; Microsoft 365 unified audit; Entra sign-in and audit logs; Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk; email headers and DNS records; Proofpoint or Abnormal Security where deployed; finance/vendor-master records; incident and fraud-management systems.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Email authentication can pass when a real mailbox is compromised. I combine headers, message trace, user activity, financial context and independent verification before declaring BEC.

## Question 242

### Interview Question

Microsoft Defender for Endpoint alerts that `WINWORD.EXE` launched PowerShell on a user's laptop shortly after the user opened an emailed document. PowerShell then made an outbound connection, but the initial file hash has no known malicious reputation. How would you determine whether this was legitimate automation, a blocked attempt or successful malware execution?

### Interview-Ready Answer

An Office application launching PowerShell is suspicious context, not proof of malware. PowerShell is a legitimate administration tool, and file reputation is only enrichment. A previously unseen hash can still be malicious, while a process name alone can produce false positives.

I would start with the Defender alert and device timeline, preserve timestamps and build the process lineage: the Office parent, PowerShell child, exact command line, account, path, hashes, subsequent processes, files and network connections. Microsoft documents `DeviceProcessEvents` as process-creation telemetry and fields including `Timestamp`, `DeviceName`, `InitiatingProcessFileName`, `FileName`, `FolderPath`, `ProcessCommandLine`, `SHA1` and `ReportId`. Microsoft notes that `SHA256` is often unpopulated in this table, so I would not assume it is available.

An illustrative hunting query is:

```kusto
DeviceProcessEvents
| where Timestamp > ago(24h)
| where InitiatingProcessFileName =~ "winword.exe"
| project Timestamp, DeviceName, InitiatingProcessFileName,
          FileName, FolderPath, ProcessCommandLine, SHA1, ReportId
| order by Timestamp asc
```

I would pivot to `DeviceNetworkEvents` and `DeviceFileEvents` or the device timeline to identify the destination, downloaded or created files and later execution. I would search the tenant for the same hash, URL, command-line fragment and behavioural chain. Hash matches help scope an exact artefact, but process relationships and repeatable behaviour are more resilient hunting evidence when an attacker changes a file hash.

I would validate whether the document and automation were expected with the user and application owner through a trusted route. If suspicious execution is confirmed, Tier 1 should escalate and request or perform device isolation and other containment only within delegated authority. I would preserve evidence before deleting files or terminating processes unless the playbook specifically authorises immediate action.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The device timeline shows Word spawning PowerShell with an encoded command. PowerShell connects to a newly observed domain, creates a DLL in a user-writable directory, and `rundll32.exe` executes it. The same DLL establishes persistence and appears on a second endpoint. The user confirms the document was unexpected.

- **Observation:** Word launched PowerShell, which made an outbound connection after an emailed document was opened.
- **Hypothesis:** The document caused script-based malware download and execution.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** The correlated process chain, encoded command, network retrieval, DLL creation and execution, persistence and second-host occurrence confirm successful malware activity.

The verdict is **confirmed malware execution with evidence of spread, requiring endpoint containment, tenant-wide scoping, email-source investigation and escalation to incident response**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Alert time, severity, affected device and logged-on user.
2. Parent-child process lineage and full command lines.
3. File paths, creation times, hashes and signing evidence where available.
4. Network destination, DNS, proxy and firewall evidence.
5. Downloaded files and subsequent process execution.
6. Registry, scheduled-task or other persistence telemetry.
7. Same hashes, domains, commands and behaviours on other endpoints.
8. Email origin, recipients, delivery and user interaction.
9. Expected automation, software deployment or administrator activity.
10. Evidence preservation, authorised isolation and escalation.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Endpoint/XDR device timeline and advanced hunting; `DeviceProcessEvents`, `DeviceNetworkEvents` and `DeviceFileEvents`; Defender for Office 365; Sentinel; DNS, proxy and firewall logs; Windows and Sysmon telemetry where configured; VirusTotal or approved reputation services for permitted indicators; asset and change records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

I do not clear an alert because a hash is unknown or confirm it from one process name. I reconstruct the execution chain, network activity, file effects, persistence and spread before deciding whether malware ran.

## Question 243

### Interview Question

A vulnerability scanner reports two findings: an internal, segmented research server with a CVSS Base score of 9.8 and an internet-facing VPN gateway with a CVSS Base score of 8.1 that is listed in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. Both asset owners request priority. As a Tier 1 analyst, how would you validate and prioritise them without treating CVSS as a complete risk score?

### Interview-Ready Answer

CVSS communicates vulnerability severity. In CVSS v4.0, the Base metrics describe intrinsic characteristics; Threat and Environmental metrics can refine the assessment, while Supplemental metrics add context. FIRST explicitly warns that a Base score measures severity, not complete organisational risk.

I would first record which CVSS version and vector the scanner used because a numeric score without its version and metrics can be misleading. I would validate the asset identity, software version, scanner evidence and whether the detection could be stale or credential-limited. Validation should use safe inventory, configuration and vendor evidence rather than attempting exploitation in production.

For prioritisation, I would combine technical severity with confirmed presence, internet exposure, reachability, business criticality, privilege required, available controls, patch or mitigation availability, threat intelligence and evidence of active exploitation. CISA's KEV catalog represents vulnerabilities known to be exploited; FIRST advises that direct exploitation evidence and KEV status should take precedence over a low EPSS score. EPSS estimates the probability of exploitation in the next 30 days, but it does not know whether the organisation is exposed or what the business impact would be.

On the stated evidence, I would normally prioritise the internet-facing KEV gateway first because it is exposed and the vulnerability is known to be exploited, even though its CVSS Base score is lower. I would still raise the 9.8 internal finding and assess whether segmentation, sensitive research data or reachable attack paths increase its priority.

Tier 1 can validate evidence, enrich the finding, assign the correct owner and severity under policy, open or update remediation tickets and monitor deadlines. Patching, service shutdown and firewall changes require asset-owner and change authority. Closure requires remediation evidence and a validation scan or equivalent technical verification; accepting risk is a documented business decision, not an analyst shortcut.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The scanner used authenticated checks and asset inventory confirms that the VPN gateway is vulnerable, internet-reachable and covered by the KEV entry. A vendor patch exists. The research server is also vulnerable but is isolated from user networks, has no known exploitation evidence and has an approved maintenance window later in the week.

- **Observation:** Two genuine vulnerabilities have different severity scores and exposure contexts.
- **Hypothesis:** The lower-scored gateway presents the more immediate intrusion path because it is externally reachable and actively exploited in the wild.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Authenticated version evidence, internet reachability, KEV inclusion and an available fix confirm the gateway as the first remediation priority; this confirms exposure and priority, not that compromise has occurred.

The verdict is **prioritise emergency remediation or approved mitigation of the internet-facing KEV gateway, while tracking the critical internal finding through its risk-based deadline and monitoring both assets for exploitation evidence**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. CVE, CVSS version, vector and source of the score.
2. Scanner method, authentication status and evidence quality.
3. Asset owner, software version and inventory accuracy.
4. Internet exposure, reachability and attack path.
5. CISA KEV status and current threat intelligence.
6. EPSS as one probability input, not a complete risk score.
7. Business service, data sensitivity and operational consequence.
8. Compensating controls and detected exploitation attempts.
9. Vendor patch, workaround, change window and accountable owner.
10. Remediation evidence, rescan, exception or formal risk acceptance.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Approved vulnerability scanner; asset inventory/CMDB; CISA KEV; NVD and vendor advisories; FIRST CVSS and EPSS; EDR, IDS/IPS, WAF and firewall evidence; SIEM; patch and configuration management; ticketing, exception and change records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

CVSS Base ranks technical severity, not complete risk. I prioritise confirmed exposure using exploitation evidence, reachability, asset consequence and controls, then verify that remediation actually worked.

## Question 244

### Interview Question

During the morning shift, Defender XDR alerts on rapid file modifications and a ransom note on a finance laptop. A file server then reports similar extensions, and the automated device-isolation action is shown as pending. Using current NIST incident-response guidance, how would you triage, prioritise and escalate this incident as a Tier 1 analyst?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat this as a potential high-impact ransomware incident because activity may be spreading from one endpoint to shared infrastructure. MITRE ATT&CK identifies data encryption for impact as T1486, but the technique label supports classification; it does not replace verification.

Current NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 supersedes Rev. 2 and aligns incident response with CSF 2.0. Govern, Identify and Protect support preparation; Detect, Respond and Recover form the incident-response cycle; lessons learned feed continuous improvement. In this scenario, Tier 1 primarily contributes to Detect and the opening of Respond by validating the incident, establishing scope, preserving evidence, assigning severity, communicating and initiating authorised containment.

I would confirm the alert state and whether isolation actually completed rather than assuming that a pending automated action succeeded. I would record the initial timeline, device, user, processes, ransom-note path, modified files, network shares and identity activity. I would immediately search for the same process, hash, extension, note name, destination and account activity across endpoints and servers.

I would escalate through the major-incident or ransomware procedure as soon as credible spread is identified. Authorised containment may include isolating affected endpoints, restricting compromised accounts, blocking confirmed infrastructure and protecting reachable backups or shares. I would avoid powering systems off, deleting artefacts or making broad network changes unless directed by the playbook or incident commander because those actions can destroy evidence or disrupt recovery.

Eradication and recovery require the incident-response, infrastructure, identity, backup, legal and business-continuity owners. Systems should be restored from verified clean sources only after the entry point, persistence and credential exposure have been addressed. Recovery validation and heightened monitoring must precede closure. Afterwards, lessons concerning detection, segmentation, backup access, communications and playbook performance should be tracked to improvement owners.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The isolation action remained pending because the laptop lost sensor connectivity. Firewall evidence shows the user's session accessing a file share, and the server records hundreds of rapid file modifications plus the same ransom-note name. A second device using the same account begins similar activity.

- **Observation:** File-encryption indicators appear on a finance endpoint and shared server while containment is incomplete.
- **Hypothesis:** Ransomware executed under the user's context and is affecting reachable resources or spreading through compromised credentials.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Correlated mass file changes, matching ransom notes, shared-resource access and equivalent activity on a second device confirm an active multi-system incident.

The verdict is **confirmed ransomware impact with ongoing spread risk, requiring immediate major-incident escalation, verified containment, identity and infrastructure scoping, evidence preservation and coordinated recovery**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Alert status, timestamps and whether automated actions completed.
2. Endpoint process lineage, command line, files and network activity.
3. Modified-file pattern, ransom note and affected directories or shares.
4. User, service-account and privileged-account activity.
5. Similar indicators and behaviours across endpoints and servers.
6. EDR sensor health and gaps in telemetry.
7. Backup reachability and signs of tampering, without modifying evidence.
8. Business service, data sensitivity, spread and operational impact.
9. Major-incident contacts, evidence log and communication record.
10. Authorised containment, recovery validation and improvement actions.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Defender XDR and Defender for Endpoint; Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk; Windows, Sysmon and file-server audit telemetry where configured; identity and Active Directory logs; firewall, DNS, proxy and network-flow evidence; backup and storage audit logs; incident-management, CMDB and business-continuity records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

For suspected ransomware, I verify impact and containment status, scope spread quickly and escalate early. Tier 1 supports Detect and Respond; eradication, recovery and organisation-wide decisions remain coordinated, authorised activities.
