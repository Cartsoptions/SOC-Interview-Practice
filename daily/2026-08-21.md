# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-08-21

## Question 13

### Interview Question

What are SPF, DKIM and DMARC, and how would you use their results during a phishing investigation?

### Interview-Ready Answer

SPF, DKIM and DMARC are complementary email-authentication controls used to reduce spoofing and help receiving systems evaluate whether messages claiming to come from a domain are legitimate.

SPF, or Sender Policy Framework, lets a domain publish which mail sources are authorised to send for that domain. DKIM, or DomainKeys Identified Mail, adds a cryptographic signature to selected message content so the recipient can verify the signature and determine whether the signed content was altered in transit. DMARC, or Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance, builds on SPF and DKIM and checks alignment with the visible From domain; it also lets the domain owner publish handling and reporting policy for messages that fail DMARC.

In a SOC investigation, I would treat SPF, DKIM and DMARC results as evidence, not as a final verdict. A phishing message can still pass SPF or DKIM if the attacker controls the sending domain. Microsoft specifically notes that SPF and DKIM alone do not require the authenticated domain to match the From address shown to the user; DMARC adds alignment checks. I would therefore review the Authentication-Results header, sender and reply-to domains, look-alike domains, message routing, URLs, attachments, threat verdicts and user interaction rather than saying 'SPF passed, so the email is safe.'

As a Tier 1 analyst, my role would usually be to interpret these authentication results and correlate them with the rest of the email evidence. Changing the organisation's SPF, DKIM or DMARC DNS records would normally be an email-security or messaging-administration responsibility unless my role explicitly authorises it.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

An accounts user reports an email that appears to come from a supplier asking for an urgent bank-detail change. The message passes SPF because it was sent from infrastructure legitimately authorised for the attacker's look-alike domain. I would compare the visible From domain with the authenticated domains, review DMARC alignment, inspect reply-to and routing information, then investigate the URL/attachment and whether other recipients received the message. If the message uses a look-alike domain, a technically valid SPF result does not make the business request trustworthy.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- `Authentication-Results` and relevant message-header evidence
- SPF result and the domain/source it actually authenticated
- DKIM result and the signing domain
- DMARC result and domain alignment with the visible From address
- Sender, reply-to and return-path differences
- Look-alike or newly observed domains
- URLs, attachments and email-security verdicts
- Other recipients and whether anyone clicked, replied or submitted credentials
- Related identity or endpoint activity if interaction occurred
- Whether escalation or remediation is required under the phishing/BEC playbook

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365/Email entity/Threat Explorer where licensed; Proofpoint or Abnormal Security where deployed; message headers; Exchange/message trace where available; SIEM; DNS/reputation sources; EDR and identity logs when user interaction occurred.

### Interview Takeaway

SPF checks authorised sending sources, DKIM validates a cryptographic message signature, and DMARC adds alignment and policy on top of SPF/DKIM. In a SOC investigation I would use those results as supporting evidence, then correlate them with the visible sender, routing, URLs, attachments and user activity before deciding whether the message is malicious.

## Question 14

### Interview Question

What is MITRE ATT&CK, and how would you use it during a real SOC investigation rather than just memorising technique IDs?

### Interview-Ready Answer

MITRE ATT&CK is a knowledge base and taxonomy of observed adversary behaviour. In ATT&CK, tactics describe why an adversary is performing an action, techniques describe how the adversary may achieve that goal, sub-techniques provide more specific behaviour, and procedures describe particular real-world implementations.

In SOC work, I would use ATT&CK as a common language for describing observed behaviour and connecting an investigation to known adversary techniques. I would not treat a single ATT&CK mapping as proof of attacker identity or as proof that an incident is confirmed. The telemetry comes first; ATT&CK helps me classify and communicate the behaviour.

For example, if an EDR alert shows PowerShell execution, I could map confirmed PowerShell behaviour to Command and Scripting Interpreter: PowerShell (T1059.001). If the same investigation then shows credential dumping or lateral movement, I could map those separately. This helps structure the timeline, understand what other evidence may be relevant, communicate clearly during escalation and identify detection gaps.

As a Tier 1 analyst, I would usually map the behaviours I can support with evidence and avoid over-mapping. MITRE itself cautions against treating ATT&CK as a checklist or trying to claim complete coverage simply because one implementation of a technique is detected.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

A workstation generates an EDR alert for suspicious PowerShell. Investigation shows an Office process spawned PowerShell, which then downloaded content and created a scheduled task. I would document the process chain and map only the behaviours supported by evidence, such as PowerShell execution and the relevant scheduled-task behaviour. I would then use ATT&CK to help structure the escalation and consider what related behaviours or data sources should be checked next, rather than jumping straight to attribution.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- The raw alert and telemetry before assigning ATT&CK mappings
- Which behaviour is directly observed versus inferred
- Relevant tactic, technique and sub-technique only when supported
- Process tree, command-line/script context, files, registry and network activity
- Related identity, persistence, credential-access or lateral-movement evidence
- Whether similar behaviour appears on other hosts/users
- Existing detections or playbooks associated with the mapped technique
- Whether the technique mapping improves escalation clarity or scope
- Whether I am accidentally using ATT&CK as proof of attribution rather than a behavioural model

### Tools / Logs

MITRE ATT&CK; ATT&CK Navigator where useful; SIEM; EDR; Windows/Linux logs; identity logs; firewall/DNS/proxy telemetry; threat-intelligence reports and incident-management records.

### Interview Takeaway

MITRE ATT&CK gives defenders a common language for adversary behaviour. I would start with the evidence, map confirmed behaviour to the relevant tactic or technique, and use that mapping to improve investigation, detection and escalation — not to replace the evidence or claim attribution.

## Question 15

### Interview Question

What is the Pyramid of Pain, and how can it help a SOC analyst prioritise indicators and detections during threat-intelligence work?

### Interview-Ready Answer

The Pyramid of Pain is a defensive concept introduced by David J. Bianco to describe how different types of indicators impose different levels of cost on an adversary when defenders successfully detect or block them. At the lower end are relatively easy-to-change indicators such as file hashes, IP addresses and domain names. Higher up are network or host artefacts, tools and, at the top, tactics, techniques and procedures (TTPs), which are generally harder for an adversary to change because they reflect how the adversary operates.

In SOC work, the model helps me understand why a hash match can be useful for immediate triage but is usually a weaker long-term detection strategy than detecting behaviour. An attacker can often rebuild a file and change its hash quickly. Changing infrastructure can take more effort. Changing tooling or established TTPs can impose substantially more operational cost.

I would not treat the Pyramid of Pain as a formal standard or as a rule that says low-level indicators are useless. Hashes, domains and IPs can still be valuable for containment, scoping and historical searches. The practical lesson is to use atomic indicators where they help, but also look for behavioural detections that survive small attacker changes.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

A malware alert contains a SHA-256 hash, an external IP and a PowerShell-based execution pattern. I would search the hash across EDR/SIEM to find exact matches and check the IP/domain for related activity. But I would also examine the behaviour: how the process was launched, what command pattern was used, what persistence or network behaviour followed and whether those behaviours can be detected independently of that exact hash or infrastructure. If the attacker recompiles the malware tomorrow, a hash-only detection may miss it while a strong behaviour-based detection could still fire.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- File hashes and where they appear in the environment
- IP addresses and domains associated with the activity
- Network and host artefacts such as URI patterns, registry changes or file paths
- Tools or malware families involved, where confidently identified
- TTPs supported by observed telemetry
- Whether an IOC is highly specific but easy for an adversary to change
- Whether a behaviour-based detection or hunt can complement the IOC
- False-positive risk and prevalence before recommending blocks or detection changes
- Whether the indicator has expired, been reassigned or become benign
- What evidence should be escalated to detection engineering or threat intelligence

### Tools / Logs

SIEM; EDR; threat-intelligence platforms; VirusTotal or other approved reputation services; firewall/DNS/proxy logs; sandbox/malware-analysis output; MITRE ATT&CK for behavioural mapping; detection-engineering tickets or playbooks.

### Interview Takeaway

The Pyramid of Pain reminds me that hashes, IPs and domains are useful but comparatively easy for attackers to change, while tools and TTPs are generally more costly to replace. As a SOC analyst I would use atomic IOCs for fast triage and scoping, then look for behavioural evidence and detections that remain useful even when the attacker changes infrastructure or recompiles malware.

## Verification Notes

- Microsoft Learn states that SPF identifies authorised sending sources for a domain, DKIM uses a domain-based digital signature to validate signed message content, and DMARC uses SPF/DKIM plus domain alignment and policy/reporting. Microsoft explicitly notes that SPF or DKIM can pass without matching the visible From domain, which is why a pass result alone does not prove a message is trustworthy.
- Microsoft Learn's July 2026 email-authentication guidance and SecOps guide describe use of `Authentication-Results`, SPF/DKIM/DMARC results, composite authentication and message-header analysis during troubleshooting/investigation.
- MITRE states that ATT&CK is a knowledge base of observed adversary behaviour. MITRE defines tactics as the 'why', techniques as the 'how', sub-techniques as more specific behaviours and procedures as specific implementations. MITRE also cautions defenders not to use ATT&CK as a simple checklist or assume one detected implementation equals complete technique coverage.
- MITRE's current Enterprise ATT&CK matrix contains 15 tactics. ATT&CK content changes over time, so analysts should use current ATT&CK pages rather than rely on memorised counts or old diagrams.
- The Pyramid of Pain is an influential defensive model created by David J. Bianco, not a NIST/MITRE standard. The original model orders indicator types from hash values through IP addresses, domains, network/host artefacts, tools and TTPs, with higher levels intended to represent greater adversary difficulty/cost when defenders can reliably act on them.
