# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-10-29

**Curriculum:** Questions 169–172 | Authenticated BEC, risk-based vulnerability prioritisation, Sentinel sign-in hunting, and public-facing application exploitation

## Question 169

### Interview Question

Accounts Payable receives a bank-detail change from a long-standing supplier. SPF, DKIM and DMARC all pass, the message appears in an existing thread, and an email-security product flags unusual payment language. How would you decide whether this is genuine or business email compromise?

### Interview-Ready Answer

SPF, DKIM and DMARC are email-authentication controls, not guarantees that the person or business request is trustworthy. SPF identifies authorised sending infrastructure, DKIM verifies a cryptographic signature over selected message content, and DMARC evaluates whether an authenticated SPF or DKIM identifier aligns with the visible Author Domain and applies the domain owner's policy. A DMARC pass therefore supports authorised use of the domain; it does not rule out a compromised supplier mailbox, malicious authorised account, deceptive subdomain, or fraudulent request sent from legitimate infrastructure.

In a SOC environment, I would preserve the original message and review the sender, reply-to, return path, originating infrastructure, authentication results, message identifiers, timestamps, thread history, URLs, attachments and delivery actions. In Microsoft Defender for Office 365, where licensed and permitted, I would use the Email entity page and Threat Explorer or Real-time detections to examine message details, recipients, related messages, URLs, attachments, threat verdicts and delivery locations. If Proofpoint or Abnormal Security is deployed, I would review the alert evidence and behavioural or relationship context presented by that product without treating its score as proof.

I would compare the request with previous legitimate supplier communications: normal contacts, wording, payment process, known domains and whether bank details have changed before. The finance or procurement team should verify the request using a trusted contact method obtained independently from the suspicious email. I would not reply to the message or call a number contained only in it.

If the request has already caused a payment, the organisation should urgently contact its bank through an official channel and invoke the fraud and incident-response process. The Tier 1 role is to preserve and correlate evidence, scope recipients and activity, initiate authorised containment and escalation, and clearly separate email authentication from business-request validation.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The message passes DMARC because it was sent from the supplier's real Microsoft 365 tenant. The supplier confirms by telephone, using a previously recorded number, that its mailbox was compromised and that its bank details have not changed. Three employees received similar requests.

- **Observation:** The message is domain-authenticated and appears in a genuine thread, but it requests an urgent bank-detail change and differs from the established payment process.
- **Hypothesis:** An attacker controls the supplier mailbox or an authorised session and is attempting payment diversion.
- **Confirmed evidence:** Independent supplier confirmation, recipient scoping and the unauthorised payment request establish BEC. The authentication pass explains why simple spoofing controls did not block it.

The verdict is **high-severity true positive — authenticated BEC from a compromised external mailbox, with payment impact still to be confirmed**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve the original message, full headers, body, attachments, URLs and message identifiers.
2. Review SPF, DKIM, DMARC and composite-authentication results without treating a pass as a benign verdict.
3. Compare sender, reply-to, return path, thread history, language, recipients and previous supplier messages.
4. Search for similar messages, other recipients, clicks, replies, forwarding and payment-related activity.
5. Verify the request through a trusted supplier contact obtained independently of the email.
6. Ask finance whether payment, bank-detail or purchase-order changes occurred, while preserving transaction evidence.
7. Invoke or request message removal, sender/domain restrictions and internal account/session containment only under the playbook.
8. Escalate confirmed or suspected payment diversion to finance, fraud, legal/privacy and incident-response teams as required.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365 Email entity and Threat Explorer or Real-time detections where licensed, Exchange message trace and audit evidence, Proofpoint or Abnormal Security where deployed, Entra sign-in and audit logs for internal identities, finance/procurement records and the incident-management platform.

### Key Interview Takeaway

SPF, DKIM and DMARC validate aspects of domain use; they do not validate the human intent or the payment request. I would combine message evidence, relationship history and independent out-of-band verification before assigning the verdict.

## Question 170

### Interview Question

A vulnerability scanner reports an 8.1 vulnerability on an internet-facing VPN appliance that appears in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, and a 9.8 vulnerability on an isolated development server with no production data or external route. Which should be prioritised, and what would a Tier 1 analyst do?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would normally prioritise the internet-facing KEV finding for immediate validation and accelerated response, while still tracking the 9.8 finding. CVSS Base measures technical severity, not organisational risk. Risk also depends on active exploitation, exposure, asset criticality, vulnerable-product confirmation, existing controls, business impact and evidence of attack activity.

CISA's KEV catalog identifies vulnerabilities known to be exploited in the wild and is intended as an input to vulnerability-management prioritisation. FIRST's CVSS v4.0 guidance says that the Base Score should be supplemented with Threat and Environmental information rather than used alone as a risk score. The KEV-listed, internet-accessible VPN appliance therefore has a stronger combination of exploitation evidence and exposure even though its Base Score is lower.

As Tier 1, I would first validate that the exact product, version and configuration are affected and that the scanner result is not stale, duplicated or based only on an unauthenticated banner. I would identify the asset owner, business role, public reachability, patch or vendor mitigation, maintenance constraints and existing compensating controls. I would then check VPN, firewall, authentication, EDR and network telemetry for exploitation indicators.

I would not install a production patch without change authority. I would escalate the internet-facing KEV through the emergency vulnerability process, recommend or request approved patching or temporary mitigation, and track it through validation. The internal 9.8 finding should receive a documented risk decision and remediation date, not be ignored. NIST describes enterprise patch management as identifying, prioritising, acquiring, installing and verifying patches, updates and upgrades.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The VPN appliance is confirmed vulnerable and internet-accessible. Firewall and appliance logs show exploit-like requests, but there is no confirmed post-exploitation activity. The development server is genuinely isolated, has no sensitive data and is already scheduled for rebuilding.

- **Observation:** One lower-CVSS vulnerability is internet-facing and known to be exploited; the higher-CVSS vulnerability has substantially lower exposure and impact in the current environment.
- **Hypothesis:** The VPN appliance is at greater near-term risk of exploitation.
- **Confirmed evidence:** Product/version validation, external reachability and KEV inclusion confirm urgent vulnerability risk. Exploit-like requests confirm targeting, not successful compromise.

The verdict is **critical remediation priority for the VPN appliance, with a separate incident investigation if post-exploitation evidence is found; the development finding remains a tracked remediation item**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Confirm the CVE, affected product, version, configuration, scanner evidence and scan freshness.
2. Check CISA KEV status, vendor advisories, supported mitigations and patch availability.
3. Validate external reachability, asset criticality, data sensitivity and business dependency.
4. Review compensating controls such as access restriction, segmentation, MFA and enhanced monitoring.
5. Search firewall, VPN, identity, EDR and network evidence for exploitation or post-exploitation behaviour.
6. Distinguish vulnerable, targeted and compromised states in the ticket.
7. Escalate approved emergency patching or mitigation and record ownership, deadline and exceptions.
8. Verify remediation through authenticated rescanning, configuration review or vendor-supported validation.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Authenticated vulnerability scanner, asset inventory/CMDB, CISA KEV catalog, vendor advisory, VPN and firewall logs, EDR/XDR, SIEM, identity telemetry, change-management and remediation-tracking records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

I would not rank vulnerabilities by CVSS alone. Active exploitation, internet exposure, asset context and evidence of attack can make a lower Base Score the more urgent risk, while every finding still requires ownership and verified closure.

## Question 171

### Interview Question

You are asked to use Microsoft Sentinel to find interactive Entra sign-in windows containing at least twenty failures and one success for the same user within fifteen minutes. Write a starting KQL query and explain what the results prove.

### Interview-Ready Answer

Assuming Microsoft Entra sign-in logs are being ingested into the documented `SigninLogs` table, I would start with a bounded candidate-generating query:

```kusto
let lookback = 24h;
let failureThreshold = 20;
SigninLogs
| where TimeGenerated >= ago(lookback)
| where IsInteractive == true
| extend Country = tostring(LocationDetails.countryOrRegion)
| summarize
    FailedAttempts = countif(ResultType != "0"),
    SuccessfulSignIns = countif(ResultType == "0"),
    SourceIPs = make_set(IPAddress, 25),
    Countries = make_set(Country, 10),
    Applications = make_set(AppDisplayName, 10),
    ConditionalAccessResults = make_set(ConditionalAccessStatus, 10)
  by UserPrincipalName, bin(TimeGenerated, 15m)
| where FailedAttempts >= failureThreshold and SuccessfulSignIns >= 1
| order by FailedAttempts desc
```

Microsoft documents `ResultType` as a string where `0` indicates success and other values are failures. It also documents `IsInteractive`, `IPAddress`, `LocationDetails`, `AppDisplayName` and `ConditionalAccessStatus`. The thresholds are analyst-selected starting points, not Microsoft defaults.

The query identifies user/time buckets containing both outcomes. It does not prove that the failures caused the success, that one attacker generated all events, or that the successful sign-in was malicious. Because the aggregation can combine different IPs, applications and failure reasons, I would pivot back to raw `SigninLogs` events for each candidate and inspect the exact timestamps, result codes, authentication details, device, application, IP, location and Conditional Access evaluation.

I would also distinguish a Conditional Access status of `success` from proof that a fresh MFA challenge occurred; the authentication details and policy evaluation must be reviewed. NAT, VPNs, user mistakes, legacy clients and token-based non-interactive activity can alter interpretation, although this query deliberately limits the initial set to interactive sign-ins.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The query finds 47 failures against one employee from several addresses, followed by a successful interactive sign-in from an unfamiliar address. The user denies the session, and audit evidence shows an unauthorised inbox rule created shortly afterward.

- **Observation:** A concentrated failure pattern and a subsequent success occur for the same user in one fifteen-minute bucket.
- **Hypothesis:** The account may have been password-sprayed or credential-stuffed and then accessed successfully.
- **Confirmed evidence:** Raw sign-in records, user denial and the unauthorised follow-on mailbox change confirm account compromise. The query alone does not establish which password or attack method succeeded.

The verdict is **high-severity true positive — confirmed account compromise after suspicious authentication activity, with the precise credential-acquisition method unconfirmed**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Validate connector health, ingestion delay, retention, time range and table coverage.
2. Open raw events and review result codes, descriptions, IPs, timestamps, applications and correlation identifiers.
3. Examine authentication methods/details, Conditional Access policies and device information.
4. Check VPN, proxy, trusted-location, travel and user-activity context.
5. Search for successful sessions and follow-on mailbox, identity, consent, file or administrative changes.
6. Contact the user through an approved channel and preserve the response.
7. Invoke or request session revocation, credential reset and account restriction under the playbook.
8. Tune the threshold and exclusions against the organisation's baseline without suppressing confirmed attack patterns.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Sentinel, `SigninLogs`, Entra sign-in and audit logs, Identity Protection where licensed, Exchange audit evidence, VPN/proxy logs, Defender XDR and the incident-management platform.

### Key Interview Takeaway

The KQL produces candidates, not verdicts. I would validate the raw events, authentication context and follow-on activity before concluding that a failure burst resulted in account compromise.

## Question 172

### Interview Question

A WAF alerts on an exploit-like request to an internet-facing application. Seconds later the application returns errors, the web-server process spawns a shell, a new script appears in the web directory and the server connects to a rare external address. How would you triage and respond as Tier 1?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat the correlated sequence as a critical suspected exploitation incident and begin the approved containment and escalation process immediately. A WAF alert alone proves only that traffic matched a rule; it may have been blocked, may be a false positive, or may not have reached vulnerable code. The host and application evidence changes the assessment.

I would preserve and correlate the WAF, load-balancer/reverse-proxy, web access, application, operating-system and EDR records using UTC timestamps, request identifiers where available, source and destination information, method, path, response status and process ancestry. I would validate whether the WAF action allowed or blocked the request and whether the application error and process execution align with the same request window.

A web-server or application process spawning a shell, writing an unauthorised script into a served directory and making a new outbound connection is strong post-exploitation evidence when change records and administrators cannot explain it. MITRE ATT&CK T1190 describes exploitation of a public-facing application for initial access and recommends multi-signal correlation between suspicious requests, application errors, shell/process execution, file writes and egress.

Within Tier 1 authority, I would isolate or request isolation of the affected workload, restrict malicious egress and the exploit path where the playbook permits, preserve the suspected web shell and volatile evidence, and notify the application owner, infrastructure team and Incident Response. Blocking one source IP is insufficient because exploitation infrastructure can change and the attacker may already have persistence.

I would scope other nodes, containers or servers behind the application; search for the script, hash, process chain, account activity and destination; determine the vulnerable component; and check secrets, service identities and data-access logs. Specialist teams should perform eradication, patching or rebuilding and controlled recovery. OWASP recommends application logging that records enough detail about when, where, who and what to support incident investigation; infrastructure logs alone may not explain application-level exploitation.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The WAF action was `allowed` in monitoring mode. EDR confirms that the application service spawned a shell which wrote the script, and the script was requested successfully from a second external address. The application owner confirms there was no deployment or diagnostic activity.

- **Observation:** An exploit-like request is followed by application errors, unauthorised child-process execution, a web-directory file write and outbound communication.
- **Hypothesis:** The public-facing application was exploited and a web shell or similar persistence mechanism was installed.
- **Confirmed evidence:** Correlated request, process, file and follow-on access evidence plus owner confirmation establish successful unauthorised exploitation. Data theft and actor attribution remain unconfirmed.

The verdict is **critical true positive — confirmed exploitation of a public-facing application with unauthorised code execution and persistence**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve the WAF alert and raw WAF, proxy/load-balancer, web, application, OS and EDR evidence.
2. Validate rule action, request time, source, destination, method, path, response and request identifiers.
3. Correlate the application error with process ancestry, command line, account, file write and network egress.
4. Confirm whether the file is served, executed, accessed externally or present on other application nodes.
5. Check recent deployments, administrator activity and approved diagnostics before confirming unauthorised change.
6. Invoke or request workload isolation, egress restriction, exploit-path mitigation and identity/secret containment under the playbook.
7. Scope peer systems, containers, credentials, data access, persistence and lateral movement.
8. Escalate a UTC-normalised timeline, affected service, evidence, containment status, business impact and gaps to Tier 2/IR.
9. Support specialist-led patching, eradication, trusted rebuilding and controlled recovery without deleting evidence.
10. Verify remediation through configuration/version checks, rescanning, detection review and monitored service restoration.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

WAF and reverse-proxy/load-balancer logs, web and application logs, EDR/XDR, Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk, file-integrity telemetry, DNS/firewall/proxy evidence, vulnerability scanner, asset inventory, deployment/change records and incident-management records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

A WAF match is only an alert. Correlated server process, file and network evidence can establish successful exploitation. I would contain quickly, preserve the full request-to-host timeline, scope the application estate and avoid unsupported claims about data theft or attribution.

- RFC 9989, published in May 2026, is the current Standards Track DMARC specification and obsoletes RFC 7489. It defines DMARC as validation of authorised use of the Author Domain through aligned authenticated identifiers and domain policy: https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9989.html
- Microsoft documents SPF, DKIM, DMARC and ARC and explains that email authentication is used against forged senders: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/defender-office-365/email-authentication-about
- Microsoft documents the Defender for Office 365 Email entity page, including message identifiers, sender details, delivery information, authentication results, URLs, attachments and similar-message views. Availability depends on licensing and permissions: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/security/office-365-security/mdo-email-entity-page
- NCSC identifies BEC/business payment fraud as tailored impersonation used to divert payments and recommends verifying important email requests using a second trusted communication method: https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/section/respond-recover/business-payment-fraud and https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/phishing
- FIRST states that CVSS v4.0 Base measures severity rather than risk and should be enriched with Threat and Environmental information: https://www.first.org/cvss/v4.0/user-guide
- CISA describes the KEV catalog as a source of vulnerabilities known to be exploited in the wild and recommends it as an input to remediation prioritisation. Its vulnerability-response playbook distinguishes remediated, mitigated and susceptible/compromised states: https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog and https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2024-08/Federal_Government_Cybersecurity_Incident_and_Vulnerability_Response_Playbooks_508C.pdf
- NIST SP 800-40 Rev. 4 defines enterprise patch management as identifying, prioritising, acquiring, installing and verifying patches, updates and upgrades: https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/40/r4/final
- Microsoft documents the `SigninLogs` fields used in Question 171 and states that `ResultType` value `0` indicates success while other values are failures: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-monitor/reference/tables/signinlogs
- MITRE ATT&CK T1190 describes exploitation of public-facing applications and a multi-signal detection strategy correlating suspicious requests, errors, server child processes, file writes and outbound activity: https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1190/
- OWASP's Logging Cheat Sheet recommends consistent application security logging and sufficient event context about when, where, who and what for detection and investigation: https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Logging_Cheat_Sheet
- All organisations, identities, addresses, messages and events in the scenarios are simulated.
