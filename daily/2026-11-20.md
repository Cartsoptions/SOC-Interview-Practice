# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-11-20

**Curriculum:** Questions 233–236 | Lookalike-domain BEC, endpoint malware process-tree analysis, risk-based vulnerability prioritisation, and ransomware containment and recovery

## Question 233

### Interview Question

An accounts-payable user reports an urgent payment-change email that appears to come from the finance director. Microsoft Defender for Office 365 shows that SPF, DKIM and DMARC passed, but the visible sender uses a newly registered lookalike domain and the Reply-To address points to a different external mailbox. How would you determine whether this is legitimate mail or business email compromise?

### Interview-Ready Answer

SPF, DKIM and DMARC are email-authentication controls, not guarantees that a sender is trustworthy. SPF checks whether a sending server is authorised for the envelope-sender domain. DKIM verifies a cryptographic signature associated with a signing domain. DMARC evaluates whether an authenticated SPF or DKIM domain aligns with the domain visible in the From address and applies the domain owner's policy. An attacker who controls a lookalike domain can configure all three correctly, so a pass can prove that the message was authorised by that lookalike domain while the business request remains fraudulent.

In a SOC environment, I would preserve the original message and use the Email entity page, Threat Explorer or Real-time detections where licensed to review the sender, Return-Path, Reply-To, Authentication-Results, sending infrastructure, Network Message ID, URLs, attachments, delivery action and user interaction. I would compare the exact domain with the organisation's approved domains and known suppliers, checking for substituted characters, extra words and recent registration. I would avoid relying on the display name.

I would search for other recipients and related messages, clicks or attachment activity. I would also determine whether this is only an external impersonation attempt or whether a real mailbox is compromised by reviewing sign-in and audit evidence, sent items, forwarding or inbox rules, delegate changes and suspicious application consent where available. A separate, trusted contact route—not a phone number or reply address in the suspicious message—should be used to verify the payment request with finance.

If the message is malicious, Tier 1 should follow the authorised playbook to quarantine or purge related messages, block indicators where appropriate, identify affected users and escalate any click, credential entry, payment or mailbox compromise. Mailbox, domain or tenant-wide changes remain administrator or incident-lead actions unless explicitly delegated.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Threat Explorer finds the message in twelve mailboxes and shows that one user clicked its payment-portal URL. The registered finance domain is `northstar-finance.co.uk`, but the message came from `northstar-finances.co.uk`. Finance confirms that no bank-detail change was requested, and the clicked page collected Microsoft 365 credentials.

- **Observation:** The message passed authentication for a lookalike domain and requested an urgent financial change.
- **Hypothesis:** An external actor is impersonating finance to steal credentials and redirect payment.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Exact-domain mismatch, finance-owner denial, campaign scope, a credential-harvesting page and recorded user interaction establish malicious impersonation.

The verdict is **confirmed lookalike-domain BEC and credential phishing; the clicked user's account must be investigated for compromise, but compromise is not assumed until identity evidence supports it**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Original message, timestamps, Network Message ID and recipients.
2. From, Reply-To, Return-Path and sending-domain differences.
3. Authentication-Results for SPF, DKIM, DMARC and composite authentication.
4. Domain spelling, registration context and organisational ownership.
5. Defender verdict, delivery action, policy override and detection technology.
6. URLs, redirects, attachments and safe detonation or reputation results.
7. Recipient clicks, downloads, replies, credential entry or payment action.
8. Similar messages across the tenant and any external forwarding.
9. Sign-ins, mailbox rules, sent mail, delegates and application consent for affected users.
10. Authorised message removal, indicator blocking, password/session response and escalation.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365 Email entity, Threat Explorer or Real-time detections; Exchange message trace and audit logs; Entra ID sign-in and audit logs; Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk; Proofpoint or Abnormal Security where deployed; DNS/domain-registration and approved threat-intelligence sources; finance change-control records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Passing SPF, DKIM and DMARC means the message authenticated for its sending domain; it does not prove that a lookalike domain or the requested payment change is legitimate. I verify identity, business context, campaign scope and user impact.

## Question 234

### Interview Question

Microsoft Defender for Endpoint shows this process sequence on a finance laptop shortly after the user opened an emailed document:

`OUTLOOK.EXE → WINWORD.EXE → powershell.exe → rundll32.exe`

The PowerShell process makes an outbound connection to a rare domain, and a new executable appears in a user-writable directory. How would you investigate the process tree without declaring every use of PowerShell or `rundll32.exe` malicious?

### Interview-Ready Answer

EDR records endpoint behaviour such as process creation, file activity and network connections. A process tree adds causal context by showing which process launched another process. Office spawning a scripting or system binary is suspicious, particularly after email delivery, but individual binaries such as PowerShell and `rundll32.exe` are legitimate Windows tools and require context.

I would begin with the alert and device timeline, preserving the timestamps, user, device, alert status and detection details. In Defender advanced hunting, `DeviceProcessEvents` contains process-creation records and `DeviceNetworkEvents` contains network-connection records. A safe starting query for the affected device is:

```kusto
let startTime = datetime(2026-11-20T06:00:00Z);
let endTime = startTime + 30m;
DeviceProcessEvents
| where Timestamp between (startTime .. endTime)
| where DeviceName =~ "FIN-LT-044"
| project Timestamp, DeviceName, AccountName, FileName, ProcessCommandLine,
          InitiatingProcessFileName, InitiatingProcessCommandLine, SHA1
| order by Timestamp asc
```

I would adapt the device and time window to the incident. I would then pivot to `DeviceNetworkEvents` for the same device and processes, inspecting `RemoteUrl`, `RemoteIP`, `RemotePort`, `ActionType`, the initiating process and command line. I would not treat a network-event label alone as proof that a payload completed; enforcement and telemetry context must be checked.

I would identify the original message and attachment, the created file's path, hash, signer and prevalence, and whether the command line is obfuscated or downloads content. I would look for persistence, credential access, additional child processes, lateral movement and similar activity on other devices. Hash reputation or VirusTotal is supporting evidence, not a sole verdict; new or private malware may have no detections, while legitimate files can be misclassified.

If the evidence indicates active compromise, I would escalate and isolate the device only under authorised procedure. Defender for Endpoint device isolation restricts network communication while retaining Defender service connectivity, but supported actions depend on platform, onboarding and permissions. I would preserve evidence before destructive remediation and coordinate any file quarantine, session revocation or reimaging with the incident lead.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The attachment hash is present only on this device. Word launches PowerShell with an encoded command, the resulting unsigned executable contacts the rare domain and creates a scheduled task. The same hash and domain then appear on a second laptop that received the same email campaign.

- **Observation:** An emailed document was followed by an unusual Office-child process chain, file creation and outbound communication.
- **Hypothesis:** The attachment initiated a malware downloader and established persistence.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Correlated email delivery, encoded execution, unsigned payload, command-and-control traffic, scheduled-task creation and repeat activity on another recipient confirm malware execution.

The verdict is **confirmed phishing-delivered malware with persistence and command-and-control activity, requiring host containment, campaign scoping and escalation**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Alert evidence, device, user, timestamps and telemetry coverage.
2. Complete parent-child process chain and command lines.
3. Original email, attachment, URL and recipient scope.
4. File path, hash, signer, prevalence and creation source.
5. Remote domain, IP, port, connection timing and action context.
6. Persistence such as scheduled tasks, services or startup changes.
7. Credential, browser, archive or discovery activity.
8. Similar hashes, domains and process chains across other devices.
9. User account and identity activity after execution.
10. Isolation authority, evidence preservation and escalation scope.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Endpoint device timeline and advanced hunting; Defender for Office 365; Microsoft Sentinel; `DeviceProcessEvents`, `DeviceNetworkEvents` and related Defender tables; Sysmon where configured; Wazuh file-integrity or malware-detection telemetry where deployed; DNS, proxy and firewall logs; approved hash-reputation and malware-analysis services.

### Key Interview Takeaway

I judge a process chain by its context and correlated effects. Office spawning a living-off-the-land binary is a strong lead; email, file, network and persistence evidence establish the verdict.

## Question 235

### Interview Question

A vulnerability scanner reports two findings. An internet-facing remote-access appliance has a CVSS score of 8.1 and appears in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue. An isolated test server has a CVSS score of 9.8, no known exploitation evidence and no route from production. Both fixes require downtime. Which should be prioritised, and what would Tier 1 contribute to the vulnerability-management lifecycle?

### Interview-Ready Answer

A CVE is a public identifier for a specific disclosed vulnerability. CVSS expresses technical severity; it is useful, but it is not a complete organisational risk score. CISA's KEV catalogue identifies vulnerabilities with evidence of exploitation in the wild. Prioritisation should therefore combine severity with active exploitation, internet exposure, asset criticality, vulnerable-product confirmation, available mitigations and business impact.

On the stated evidence, I would prioritise the internet-facing remote-access appliance even though its CVSS score is lower. It is exposed and the vulnerability is known to be exploited. I would still track the 9.8 finding and verify its isolation rather than dismissing it.

I would first validate that the detected product and version are actually vulnerable and that the scanner result is current, avoiding a patch decision based only on a scanner title. I would check the vendor advisory, CVE record, KEV entry, asset owner, exposure, compensating controls and whether exploitation indicators already exist. If immediate patching is not possible, the incident or vulnerability lead may approve mitigations such as disabling an affected feature, restricting access or increasing monitoring; these are governed changes, not unilateral Tier 1 actions.

The practical lifecycle is discovery, validation, prioritisation, assignment, remediation or mitigation, verification and closure or documented risk acceptance. Tier 1 can enrich findings, identify exposed assets, search logs for exploitation, open or update tickets, monitor deadlines and confirm that rescanning occurred. The asset owner and change authority normally implement patches, while risk owners approve exceptions.

Closure requires evidence: the affected version is removed or an approved mitigation is effective, the asset has been rescanned, and any exception has an owner and expiry. A scanner showing the finding once is an observation; successful exploitation or a matching KEV entry changes urgency, while a clean rescan alone may need corroboration if the asset was offline or unauthenticated.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The scanner confirms the vulnerable version on the external appliance. The KEV entry is current, firewall logs show exploitation attempts against the service and the vendor provides an emergency fixed release. The test server is segmented and has no production data or reachable route.

- **Observation:** Two vulnerabilities have different severity, exposure and exploitation contexts.
- **Hypothesis:** The internet-facing KEV finding represents the more immediate compromise path.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Vulnerable-version confirmation, KEV status, external exposure and matching exploit attempts establish urgent risk; segmentation evidence reduces, but does not eliminate, the test server's risk.

The verdict is **remediate or mitigate the exposed KEV finding first under emergency change control, hunt for compromise, then address the isolated critical finding within its risk-based deadline**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. CVE, vendor advisory and affected product/version.
2. Scanner authentication, evidence, timestamp and confidence.
3. CVSS version, vector and source—not only the numeric score.
4. CISA KEV status and remediation due date where applicable.
5. Internet exposure, network paths and asset criticality.
6. Existing controls, patch availability and vendor mitigations.
7. Firewall, IDS, EDR and application evidence of exploitation.
8. Asset owner, ticket, change window, SLA and exception status.
9. Post-change rescan and product-version confirmation.
10. Documented closure, risk acceptance, owner and expiry date.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Tenable, Nessus or another authorised vulnerability scanner; CISA KEV; NVD and vendor advisories; CMDB and asset inventory; firewall, IDS/IPS, WAF, EDR and application logs; Microsoft Defender Vulnerability Management where deployed; SIEM, ticketing and change-management systems.

### Key Interview Takeaway

CVSS tells me technical severity, not the complete remediation order. I combine confirmed exposure, exploitation evidence, KEV status, asset importance and compensating controls, then verify that remediation actually worked.

## Question 236

### Interview Question

Wazuh file-integrity alerts show rapid modifications and extensions changing across a shared drive. Microsoft Defender for Endpoint simultaneously reports suspicious encryption behaviour on two workstations, while Sentinel shows SMB activity from one of those hosts to several file servers. How would you handle the first hour of a suspected ransomware incident?

### Interview-Ready Answer

File-integrity monitoring detects changes to configured files and directories. It can reveal unusual creation, modification or deletion patterns, but rapid changes alone do not prove ransomware; legitimate software deployment, backup, synchronisation or bulk processing can look similar. Wazuh documents that its FIM module monitors specified local paths and can be integrated with threat-intelligence or response components, but the exact coverage and automated actions depend on configuration.

My first priorities are safety, containment, evidence preservation and rapid escalation. I would declare a suspected high-severity incident through the approved process, record the initial timeline and identify affected hosts, users, shares and critical services. I would correlate Wazuh changes with Defender process trees, file events, SMB activity, Windows logs and storage or backup telemetry.

If encryption is active or spreading, I would request or perform authorised isolation of affected endpoints and restrict the malicious network path. CISA recommends determining impacted systems and immediately isolating them. Powering systems down is generally reserved for situations where network disconnection is not possible because shutdown can destroy volatile evidence. Broadly disconnecting servers, disabling accounts or taking subnets offline requires incident-command authority and business-impact coordination.

I would preserve representative ransom notes, file names, hashes, process evidence, volatile data where the specialist team can collect it, and short-retention logs. I would identify the initial access path, affected credentials, persistence, command and control, lateral movement and possible exfiltration. MITRE ATT&CK maps data encryption for impact to T1486, but an ATT&CK mapping describes behaviour rather than proving attribution or a specific ransomware family.

During eradication and recovery, senior responders and system owners remove the entry point and persistence, rotate affected credentials, rebuild or clean systems under the recovery plan, and restore from validated backups. Restoration should follow business priorities and be monitored for recurrence. NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 treats incident response as part of broader cybersecurity risk management across preparation, detection, response and recovery; post-incident findings should improve controls, backups, detections and playbooks.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Defender shows an unsigned process encrypting files and deleting recovery-related artefacts. Wazuh records hundreds of changes in monitored directories, the same endpoint writes renamed files over SMB to two servers, and users confirm ransom notes. Backup administrators confirm that offline recovery points remain intact.

- **Observation:** Multiple controls report correlated mass file changes, encryption behaviour and lateral file-share activity.
- **Hypothesis:** Ransomware is actively encrypting endpoint and shared data and may spread further.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** The encryption process, ransomware notes, correlated SMB writes and matching file changes confirm active ransomware impact.

The verdict is **confirmed ransomware incident requiring immediate coordinated containment, enterprise scoping, evidence preservation and recovery under incident command**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Earliest alert, affected hosts, users and reliable timestamps.
2. FIM coverage, changed paths, volume, extensions and exclusions.
3. Defender process tree, command line, file hash and signer.
4. SMB source, destinations, shares and successful write activity.
5. Ransom notes, encrypted samples and affected critical services.
6. Identity activity, privileged accounts and lateral movement.
7. Initial access, persistence, command and control and possible exfiltration.
8. Backup status without connecting known-good backups to compromised systems.
9. Authorised isolation, network restrictions and stakeholder notifications.
10. Evidence preservation, recovery priorities, monitoring and lessons learned.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Wazuh FIM and alert telemetry; Microsoft Defender for Endpoint and Defender XDR; Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk; Windows Security and Sysmon where configured; SMB, file-server, firewall, DNS and proxy logs; identity telemetry; backup and storage-platform audit; incident-management and asset-criticality records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

In the first hour, I confirm whether encryption is active, contain the spread within authority, preserve evidence and build a reliable scope. Recovery begins only after the entry path and persistence are addressed and backups are validated.

**SPF, DKIM and DMARC passed—so the email must be safe. Right?**

Not necessarily.

An attacker can register a convincing lookalike domain and configure its email authentication correctly. The result is a message that passes SPF, DKIM and DMARC for the attacker's domain while still impersonating your finance director.

In a SOC investigation, I would compare the exact From domain, Reply-To and Return-Path; review the Authentication-Results header; inspect URLs and attachments; search for other recipients and clicks; and verify the payment request through a trusted contact route.

The key distinction is simple: authentication can show that a domain authorised the message. It does not prove that the domain—or the business request—is legitimate.
