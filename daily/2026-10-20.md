# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-10-20

**Curriculum:** Questions 141–144 | Vulnerability prioritisation, scanner validation, Defender Vulnerability Management hunting, confirmed exploitation and incident response

## Question 141

### Interview Question

A vulnerability scanner reports two findings. Finding A has a CVSS Base score of 9.8 on an internet-facing customer portal and appears in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue. Finding B has a CVSS Base score of 8.1 on an internal identity-management server, is not in KEV, and has no observed exploitation. Which would you prioritise, and how would you explain the difference between a CVE and CVSS?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would initially prioritise Finding A for urgent validation and risk reduction because it combines high technical severity, direct external exposure and evidence that the vulnerability has been exploited in the wild. I would not ignore Finding B: an identity-management server is a high-value asset, so I would assess it in parallel and escalate quickly if exposure, exploitability or compensating-control evidence changed its risk.

A CVE is a common identifier for a publicly disclosed vulnerability. It lets vendors, scanners and defenders refer to the same issue; the identifier is not a severity score and does not prove that a particular asset is affected. CVSS is a scoring framework for describing vulnerability severity. In CVSS v4.0, the Base metrics describe intrinsic characteristics, while Threat and Environmental metrics can add current exploit and organisation-specific context. I would confirm the CVSS version and vector rather than compare numbers without knowing how they were produced.

In a SOC environment, I would treat CVSS as one input, not an automatic patch queue. I would also consider CISA KEV status, vendor advisories, exploit prerequisites, asset exposure, asset criticality, privileges obtainable, data at risk, compensating controls, affected population and any attack telemetry. CISA describes KEV as an authoritative catalogue of vulnerabilities known to have been exploited in the wild and recommends using it as an input to vulnerability-prioritisation decisions.

As Tier 1, I would validate the alert, collect the risk context, open or update the case and route it to the vulnerability owner or incident team under the organisation's service levels. The system owner or engineering team normally tests and deploys the patch or mitigation. I would not make an unapproved production change.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The portal's version and vendor advisory confirm that Finding A applies. External attack-surface records show the service is reachable from the internet, and recent WAF logs contain probes for the affected route but no verified successful exploitation. The identity server is reachable only from a restricted management network and has no related alerts.

- **Observation:** Finding A is present on an externally reachable asset, is in KEV and is being probed. Finding B affects a critical internal asset but has more restricted exposure and no observed exploitation.
- **Hypothesis:** Finding A is more likely to be exploited soon; Finding B could still have severe impact if an attacker gains the required access.
- **Confirmed evidence:** Version and advisory matching confirm exposure to each vulnerability. KEV confirms known exploitation in the wild, not exploitation of this organisation. A confirmed incident requires local evidence that exploitation succeeded or caused unauthorised activity.

The defensible initial verdict is **Finding A first for emergency validation and remediation, while Finding B remains a high-priority tracked risk based on identity-system criticality**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Confirm the exact asset, owner, environment, internet or network exposure, business service and data sensitivity.
2. Validate the finding against product name, version/build, vendor advisory and scanner evidence; check for duplicate or stale asset records.
3. Record the CVE identifier, CVSS version and vector, severity, KEV status, vendor mitigation and patch availability.
4. Check WAF, IDS/IPS, proxy, firewall, EDR and application logs for attempted or successful exploitation and follow-on behaviour.
5. Review segmentation, authentication, WAF/IPS coverage and other compensating controls without treating them as proof that the risk is removed.
6. Scope how many systems are affected and whether privileged, identity, public-facing or safety-critical assets are involved.
7. Escalate under the vulnerability or incident playbook with evidence, business context, remediation owner, due date and any immediate mitigation request.
8. Track the finding through approved remediation and validation rather than closing it when a ticket is merely assigned.

### Tools / Logs

Authenticated vulnerability scanner, asset inventory/CMDB, vendor security advisory, CISA KEV, CVE/CVSS records, WAF and IDS/IPS logs, firewall/proxy telemetry, EDR, Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk, change/patch-management records and the case-management system.

### Interview Takeaway

A CVE identifies the vulnerability; CVSS describes severity. I would prioritise with severity plus exploitation evidence, exposure, asset criticality, controls and local telemetry, then track the risk through validated remediation.

## Question 142

### Interview Question

An authenticated vulnerability scan still reports a critical missing security update on a production server, but the server team says the update was installed yesterday. How would you decide whether this is an unresolved vulnerability, a failed patch, or a stale/false-positive finding?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would keep the finding open while I reconcile the scanner's evidence with trustworthy endpoint and patch evidence. A change ticket marked complete is not proof that the target reached the required secure state, while a scanner result is not infallible. Common explanations include a failed or partial installation, a pending reboot, the wrong host or cluster node being checked, inaccurate software inventory, unsuccessful scan authentication, stale data, supersedence logic or a scanner plugin that needs review.

I would first confirm identity: hostname, IP, device identifier, operating system, instance or cluster role and scan timestamp. I would check whether the scan authenticated successfully because an unauthenticated or partially authenticated scan may have weaker evidence. I would then compare the installed product or OS build with the vendor advisory and the update's documented applicability, including prerequisites, supersedence and reboot requirements.

I would ask the server team for objective installation evidence such as the platform's update history, package-manager or software-inventory result, deployment-system status and change record. I would not rely on a single screenshot or the ticket state. If the correct secure version is present, I would request a targeted authenticated rescan after data refresh. If the rescan still flags the asset, I would preserve the plugin/finding output and escalate to vulnerability-management or scanner engineering for rule review rather than suppressing the result myself.

NIST treats enterprise patching as preventive maintenance and recommends an organisational strategy that operationalises patching and reduces risk. The important lifecycle is identify, assess, remediate or mitigate, verify the outcome and maintain records. Tier 1 can collect and correlate evidence; the asset owner normally performs the production change, and authorised vulnerability-management staff decide on formal exception or false-positive handling.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The deployment console reports success at 22:10, but the server has not restarted. The vendor advisory states that the update takes effect after a reboot. Local inventory still shows the vulnerable build, and the scanner authenticated successfully at 05:30.

- **Observation:** The deployment job completed, but the secure build is not active; a required reboot remains outstanding.
- **Hypothesis:** The patch files may be staged but the vulnerability remains effective until the authorised restart completes.
- **Confirmed evidence:** The vendor's applicability guidance, endpoint build and successful authenticated scan support that the server has not yet reached the remediated state.

The verdict is **true positive — remediation incomplete**, not a scanner false positive. I would escalate the pending production action and retain any approved compensating controls until validation succeeds.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Match the scanner asset to the actual server using stable identifiers, not only a reused IP address or short hostname.
2. Confirm scan time, content/plugin version, authentication/credential status and the evidence returned by the check.
3. Compare the installed build or package version with the vendor's affected and fixed versions.
4. Review patch-deployment results, update/package history, installation errors, prerequisites, supersedence and restart requirements.
5. Check whether the asset is a cluster member, image, container host or load-balanced node that could explain inconsistent results.
6. Review current exposure and compensating controls; raise priority if the asset is public-facing, critical or showing exploit activity.
7. Request the authorised remedial action, then run or request a targeted authenticated rescan after the endpoint and scanner data have refreshed.
8. Close only when the secure state is evidenced and the validation result is recorded; route disputed detections through the formal exception/false-positive process.

### Tools / Logs

Authenticated vulnerability scanner, endpoint software/build inventory, package or OS update history, patch-deployment console, vendor advisory, CMDB, EDR device inventory, change records, restart/uptime evidence, service-management ticket and targeted rescan results.

### Interview Takeaway

I would reconcile asset identity, authenticated scan evidence, installed build and vendor requirements. “Deployment succeeded” is not the same as “risk removed”; I would verify the secure state with a rescan before closure.

## Question 143

### Interview Question

Your team uses Microsoft Defender Vulnerability Management. You are asked to identify the high- and critical-severity software vulnerabilities affecting the most devices so analysts can add asset and exploit context. What KQL would you use, where would you run it, and what can the output prove?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would run the query in **Microsoft Defender XDR Advanced Hunting**, where `DeviceTvmSoftwareVulnerabilities` contains device-level software vulnerability data. Microsoft documents that this TVM table is visible in Microsoft Sentinel for schema and query-validation purposes but is not ingested there by default, so a query against it in Sentinel can return no results unless the organisation has created a separate supported ingestion path.

```kusto
DeviceTvmSoftwareVulnerabilities
| where VulnerabilitySeverityLevel in~ ("High", "Critical")
| summarize
    AffectedDevices = dcount(DeviceId),
    DeviceNames = make_set(DeviceName, 100)
    by CveId,
       SoftwareVendor,
       SoftwareName,
       SoftwareVersion,
       VulnerabilitySeverityLevel,
       RecommendedSecurityUpdate,
       RecommendedSecurityUpdateId
| order by AffectedDevices desc
```

The query filters documented severity values, groups the records by vulnerability and software context, counts distinct affected devices and retains a bounded set of device names for triage. The named columns are documented for `DeviceTvmSoftwareVulnerabilities`.

The result proves that Defender Vulnerability Management currently associates those software vulnerabilities with the returned devices, subject to sensor coverage and data freshness. It does **not** prove internet exposure, successful exploitation, malicious activity or remediation priority. Ordering by affected-device count is a workload view, not a risk score. I would enrich the results with asset criticality, exposure, KEV or other exploit evidence, security alerts, recommended remediation and business ownership.

I would also inspect the product's current schema in the portal before operational use. Queries can require adjustment as data availability, licensing and schema support differ by tenant. I would test the query over a known time/data set and document any coverage gap.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The query returns one critical CVE on 180 standard laptops and another critical CVE on two internet-facing payment servers. The first row appears larger because the query orders by affected-device count.

- **Observation:** Defender associates the first vulnerability with more devices, while the second affects fewer but more exposed and business-critical systems.
- **Hypothesis:** The two-server finding may require faster action despite its smaller device count because exposure and potential business impact are greater.
- **Confirmed evidence:** The table confirms assessed device/software vulnerability associations. CMDB and attack-surface evidence confirm the payment-server role and exposure; KEV/vendor data and local security telemetry are separate evidence sources.

The correct triage decision is **do not patch by row count alone**. Add risk context and route both findings under the applicable remediation service levels.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Run the query in Defender XDR Advanced Hunting and confirm the table has current data for the relevant device population.
2. Review the CVE, product/version, severity, recommended update and distinct affected-device count.
3. Map devices to asset owner, business function, criticality, exposure, privilege and data sensitivity.
4. Check KEV/vendor exploit information, patch or mitigation availability and applicable security controls.
5. Correlate with Defender alerts, process/network activity and SIEM logs for signs of exploitation; vulnerability presence alone is not an incident.
6. Identify stale, duplicate, offboarded or poorly covered devices and record telemetry limitations.
7. Create scoped remediation work with owners and due dates, and escalate public-facing or actively exploited exposure urgently.
8. Rerun the query or targeted assessment after remediation and retain evidence that the affected population decreased as expected.

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender XDR Advanced Hunting, `DeviceTvmSoftwareVulnerabilities`, Defender Vulnerability Management recommendations and device inventory, CMDB/asset criticality, CISA KEV and vendor advisories, Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk for correlated security telemetry, change tickets and remediation dashboards.

### Interview Takeaway

I would use Defender XDR Advanced Hunting to measure affected devices, then add exploit, exposure and business context. The query finds assessed vulnerability exposure; it does not by itself prove compromise or decide remediation priority.

## Question 144

### Interview Question

An internet-facing IIS server has a KEV-listed vulnerability. The WAF records exploit-like requests to the affected route, Defender for Endpoint shows `w3wp.exe` spawning an unexpected command shell, a new script is written to the web directory, and the server makes a new outbound connection. How would you distinguish vulnerability remediation from incident response and handle this as Tier 1?

### Interview-Ready Answer

The vulnerability finding is now only one part of the case. A vulnerable version plus an exploit request shows exposure and attempted exploitation; it does not alone prove success. The correlated endpoint sequence — the web-server worker spawning an unexpected shell, a new script appearing in the web directory and new outbound activity — is strong local evidence of unauthorised execution. Unless an approved administrative action explains it, I would treat this as a confirmed compromise and escalate immediately to incident response.

Vulnerability management asks whether the weakness exists, how to reduce the risk and how to validate remediation. Incident response asks what happened, which assets and accounts are affected, how to contain ongoing harm, how to eradicate attacker access and how to recover safely. Applying the patch may close the initial access path, but it does not remove web shells, scheduled tasks, stolen credentials, added accounts or lateral movement. Conversely, isolating the server can contain the incident but does not permanently remediate the vulnerability.

I would build a time-normalised timeline from WAF, application/web, EDR, firewall/proxy, DNS, identity and change records. I would preserve the request details, process tree, command line, file path/hash, outbound destination and relevant logs. Under an authorised playbook, I would isolate the server or remove it from external service, block confirmed malicious indicators and protect affected accounts or secrets. If Tier 1 lacks those permissions, I would request those actions urgently and record the owner, approval and outcome.

I would scope other servers with the vulnerable product, the same requests, file/hash/path, commands, destination, account or persistence behaviour. MITRE ATT&CK T1190, Exploit Public-Facing Application, is an appropriate behaviour mapping when the evidence supports exploitation of the public-facing service; it does not identify the threat actor or replace the incident verdict.

For recovery, higher-tier responders and system owners should remove persistence or rebuild from a trusted source, rotate credentials/secrets where exposure is established or reasonably suspected under policy, apply the vendor fix or mitigation, verify configuration and sensor health, and restore service under change control. I would support validation with a targeted rescan plus monitoring for the original and related behaviours. NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 places incident response within CSF 2.0's Detect, Respond and Recover activities and emphasises continuous improvement across the wider risk-management lifecycle.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The change calendar contains no approved maintenance, the child-shell command downloads a second file, and the destination is not used by any other managed server. A hunt finds the new script on this server only and no confirmed lateral movement yet.

- **Observation:** A KEV-listed weakness is present; exploit traffic is followed by unauthorised shell execution, file creation and outbound communication on the target.
- **Hypothesis:** The public-facing vulnerability was used for initial access and the attacker attempted to establish further control.
- **Confirmed evidence:** Local endpoint and network evidence confirms unauthorised execution and server compromise. The exact initial-access mechanism is strongly supported but should be finalised through timeline and forensic analysis. Data theft, credential theft, persistence scope and actor identity remain unconfirmed until separately evidenced.

The initial verdict is **true positive — confirmed server compromise, likely exploitation of the public-facing application, with scope and impact still under investigation**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Confirm the alert timestamps/time zones, asset identity, business criticality, internet exposure, vulnerable version and vendor guidance.
2. Preserve raw WAF/web requests, response/status data, EDR process tree and command line, file metadata/hashes, network events and relevant identity/change records.
3. Correlate the exploit request with process creation, file writes and outbound connections; identify gaps rather than filling them with assumptions.
4. Check for web shells, persistence, new or altered accounts, credential access, discovery, lateral movement, archive/collection and exfiltration indicators.
5. Scope the vulnerability and observed behaviours across peer servers and the wider estate.
6. Invoke or request authorised containment, including removal from external service or endpoint isolation, indicator blocking and account/secret protection where justified.
7. Escalate to Tier 2/IR with the timeline, confirmed facts, hypotheses, affected assets, actions taken, evidence locations and decisions required.
8. Track both workstreams: incident containment/eradication/recovery and vulnerability patching/mitigation.
9. Validate recovery through trusted rebuild or verified eradication, patch/configuration evidence, targeted rescan, service-owner testing, restored telemetry and heightened monitoring.
10. Feed lessons into WAF/EDR detections, asset and exposure inventory, patch service levels, playbooks and control improvements.

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Endpoint/XDR device timeline and advanced hunting, WAF and IIS/web logs, firewall/proxy/DNS telemetry, Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk, Wazuh where deployed, authenticated vulnerability scanner, CISA KEV, vendor advisory, identity/audit logs, CMDB, change records and incident case management.

### Interview Takeaway

Patching the vulnerability closes a weakness; it does not clean a compromised server. I would preserve and correlate evidence, contain within authority, scope behaviours across the estate, escalate the confirmed compromise and validate both secure recovery and vulnerability remediation.

## Verification Notes

- FIRST's CVSS v4.0 guidance states that the Base score represents intrinsic vulnerability characteristics and recommends adding Threat and Environmental context for organisation-specific risk decisions. Scores should be interpreted with their version and vector.
- CISA describes KEV as an authoritative catalogue of vulnerabilities known to have been exploited in the wild and recommends it as an input to vulnerability-management prioritisation. KEV inclusion does not prove that a particular organisation was exploited.
- NIST SP 800-40 Rev. 4 treats enterprise patch management as preventive maintenance and recommends an enterprise strategy that operationalises patching and improves risk reduction. The validation steps here are practical applications of that lifecycle, not a claim that one scanner or patch product behaves universally.
- Microsoft documents the columns used in Question 143 for `DeviceTvmSoftwareVulnerabilities`. Microsoft also states that this TVM table is not ingested into Sentinel by default; it is queried in Defender XDR Advanced Hunting unless an organisation builds another supported ingestion path.
- The KQL in Question 143 is deliberately limited to documented fields. It counts Defender-assessed device/vulnerability associations and does not claim internet exposure, exploit availability or compromise.
- MITRE ATT&CK T1190 describes exploitation of an internet-facing host or system for initial access or persistence. The mapping is applied only after local evidence supports exploitation; it does not establish attribution.
- NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 was finalised on 3 April 2025 and supersedes Rev. 2. It integrates incident-response recommendations with CSF 2.0 and frames incident response through Detect, Respond and Recover, supported by broader Govern, Identify and Protect activities.
- The IIS artefact in Question 144 is simulated. A web worker spawning a command shell is suspicious context, not universal proof; the verdict relies on the correlated unauthorised process, file and network evidence and absence of an approved change.
