# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-10-02

## Question 105

### Interview Question

What are SPF, DKIM and DMARC, and how would you use their results when investigating a suspicious email?

### Interview-Ready Answer

SPF, DKIM and DMARC are complementary email-authentication controls, but I would not treat any one of them as a complete phishing verdict.

SPF identifies which sending sources are authorised for the envelope-sender domain. DKIM uses a cryptographic signature so the receiving system can validate signed message content and the signing domain. DMARC adds alignment: it checks whether the domain authenticated by SPF or DKIM aligns with the domain visible to the user in the From header, and it allows a domain owner to publish a policy for authentication failures.

In a SOC environment, I would inspect the Authentication-Results header and the email-security platform's verdicts alongside the visible sender, envelope sender, DKIM signing domain, URLs, attachments, sending infrastructure and user context.

A message that fails DMARC can be suspicious, but legitimate forwarding or message-modifying services can complicate authentication. Equally, a message that passes SPF, DKIM and DMARC is not automatically safe: an attacker can use a legitimately authenticated attacker-controlled domain or a compromised legitimate account. Authentication helps answer whether the sender is authorised for a domain; it does not prove the message's intent is benign.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

A user reports an invoice email that displays a supplier's name. I would compare the visible From domain with the MAIL FROM and DKIM signing domains, review SPF/DKIM/DMARC results, inspect the URL or attachment, check whether the sender/domain is expected for that supplier, and look for similar messages across the organisation.

If DMARC passes but the domain is a newly registered lookalike, the authentication result does not clear the email. It may simply mean the attacker correctly configured authentication for the lookalike domain.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Authentication-Results: SPF, DKIM, DMARC and, in Microsoft 365, composite authentication where available.
- Visible From address/domain versus MAIL FROM/Return-Path and DKIM signing domain.
- DMARC alignment and policy context.
- Sender IP/infrastructure and domain reputation/context.
- URLs, attachments, subject and social-engineering indicators.
- Whether the sender/domain is normal for the recipient or business relationship.
- Similar messages, recipients and reported emails.
- Email-security verdicts and delivery location/action.

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Office 365 / Defender XDR, Threat Explorer where licensed, Proofpoint or Abnormal Security where deployed, message headers, DNS records, SIEM, proxy/DNS logs and threat-intelligence sources.

### Interview Takeaway

"SPF checks authorised sending sources, DKIM validates a cryptographic signature, and DMARC adds From-domain alignment and policy. In a SOC investigation I use those results as evidence, not as a standalone phishing verdict."

## Question 106

### Interview Question

What is Business Email Compromise (BEC), and why can it be harder to detect than a normal malware phishing email?

### Interview-Ready Answer

BEC is a social-engineering attack in which an adversary impersonates or compromises a trusted business identity to manipulate a victim into actions such as transferring money, changing payment details or disclosing sensitive information. A BEC message may contain no malware and no obviously malicious attachment.

That makes it important for a Tier 1 analyst to investigate identity, conversation and business context rather than relying only on malware detections. I would examine whether the sender is external or internal, whether the display name or domain imitates a trusted person, authentication results, reply-to differences, unusual wording or urgency, mailbox/account alerts, sign-in activity where relevant, and whether the requested business action is unusual.

I would also distinguish spoofing from account compromise. A spoofed executive address and a genuinely compromised executive mailbox create different evidence trails and response requirements.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Accounts Payable receives a message apparently from a senior executive asking for an urgent supplier bank-account change. There is no attachment and the URL scan is clean.

I would not close the alert simply because there is no malware. I would inspect sender and reply-to details, authentication, message history, whether the request matches established payment-change procedures, and whether the supposed sender's account shows suspicious sign-in or mailbox activity. Financial verification should follow the organisation's approved out-of-band process rather than replying to the suspicious email.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- SenderFrom, MAIL FROM/Return-Path and Reply-To relationships.
- SPF, DKIM, DMARC/composite-authentication results.
- Display-name and lookalike-domain indicators.
- Message thread/history and whether the request is normal.
- Entra/identity sign-ins and mailbox security alerts if an internal account may be compromised.
- Inbox/forwarding-rule or OAuth-related evidence where relevant and available.
- Other recipients or similar messages.
- Whether a payment/data action has already occurred.
- Escalation to the fraud/finance/incident-response process when appropriate.

### Tools / Logs

Defender for Office 365 / Defender XDR, Proofpoint, Abnormal Security, Exchange/Message Trace, Entra sign-in logs, SIEM, mailbox audit evidence where available, DNS/WHOIS/threat intelligence as appropriate.

### Interview Takeaway

"BEC may have no malware at all. I focus on sender identity, authentication, account and mailbox evidence, business context, and whether the requested action is legitimate. I do not rely on an attachment or URL verdict to decide whether a BEC attempt is malicious."

## Question 107

### Interview Question

Explain what this Microsoft Defender XDR Advanced Hunting query does and what it does *not* prove:

```kusto
EmailEvents
| where Timestamp > ago(24h)
| where EmailDirection == "Inbound"
| summarize Messages=count(),
            Recipients=dcount(RecipientEmailAddress)
            by SenderFromAddress, SenderIPv4, DeliveryAction
| order by Recipients desc
```

### Interview-Ready Answer

This query searches the Defender XDR `EmailEvents` table for inbound email events recorded in the last 24 hours.

It groups the results by the visible From address (`SenderFromAddress`), the last detected relaying mail server's IPv4 address (`SenderIPv4`) and `DeliveryAction`. For each group, `count()` gives the number of matching email-event records and `dcount(RecipientEmailAddress)` gives an approximate distinct count of recipients. It then orders the results so groups reaching the most distinct recipients appear first.

Microsoft documents these fields in the EmailEvents advanced-hunting schema. Microsoft also documents delivery-action values such as Delivered, Junked, Blocked and Replaced.

The query is useful for identifying senders/infrastructure that reached many recipients, but it does not prove phishing. Bulk legitimate senders can also produce high recipient counts, and `SenderIPv4` is mail-relay context rather than automatically the attacker's endpoint IP.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The result shows one sender reaching 180 recipients, with many messages delivered. I would pivot to the underlying email records, inspect the subject, authentication and threat/detection fields, correlate URLs through `EmailUrlInfo` using `NetworkMessageId`, review sender/domain context, and determine whether recipients clicked or whether post-delivery remediation occurred.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

**Observation:** One sender/IP/action grouping appears across many recipients.

**Hypothesis:** This could be a phishing campaign or other unwanted bulk mail.

**Confirmed Evidence:** I need the raw message evidence, authentication, content/URL/attachment context, security verdicts and relevant user/post-delivery activity before calling it malicious.

- Raw EmailEvents rows and NetworkMessageId.
- SenderFromAddress versus SenderMailFromAddress/domain.
- Authentication and filtering verdicts where available.
- Subject, recipients, delivery action/location and detection methods.
- EmailUrlInfo / EmailAttachmentInfo correlation by NetworkMessageId.
- Post-delivery actions in EmailPostDeliveryEvents where available.
- Click/browser/proxy/endpoint evidence if assessing user impact.

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender XDR Advanced Hunting, Defender for Office 365, EmailEvents, EmailUrlInfo, EmailAttachmentInfo, EmailPostDeliveryEvents, Sentinel where integrated, proxy/DNS/browser and EDR telemetry.

### Interview Takeaway

"This hunt surfaces high-volume inbound sender patterns. It is a triage lead, not a phishing verdict. I pivot to the raw messages and correlate authentication, content, URLs, delivery and user activity."

## Question 108

### Interview Question

A user reports that they clicked a link in a suspicious email and entered their Microsoft 365 credentials. They say nothing downloaded. How would you triage this as a Tier 1 SOC analyst?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would treat this primarily as a potential identity compromise, not dismiss it because no file downloaded.

First I would establish the timeline: when the email arrived, when the link was clicked, when credentials were entered, and what identity activity occurred afterwards. I would preserve the message identifiers and URL evidence, identify other recipients, and determine whether the same campaign affected anyone else.

I would then pivot to the user's identity telemetry. I would review sign-ins and relevant risk/conditional-access/MFA evidence available to the SOC, looking for new or unusual sessions, source locations/infrastructure, devices and post-authentication activity. I would also check Defender/email and endpoint telemetry for any additional behaviour, because a phishing link can lead to credential theft, token/consent abuse or malware even when the user did not notice a download.

MITRE ATT&CK classifies spearphishing links as T1566.002 and its current detection guidance recommends correlating inbound malicious links with user navigation and subsequent browser/process or identity activity.

If evidence indicates credential or session compromise, I would escalate immediately and follow the organisation's identity-containment playbook. Depending on authorised procedures, response may include revoking sessions/tokens, resetting credentials, requiring/re-registering authentication factors when appropriate, blocking malicious infrastructure/messages, and scoping other recipients. I would not claim to perform those administrative actions unless my Tier 1 permissions explicitly allow them.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

A user clicked a fake Microsoft 365 sign-in page at 09:12 and entered credentials. At 09:18, identity logs show a new sign-in/session from infrastructure inconsistent with the user's normal context, followed by unusual mailbox or cloud activity.

That chain is materially stronger than the email report alone:

**phishing email → user click → credential submission → anomalous authentication/session → post-login activity**

The investigation priority rises sharply because there is evidence consistent with successful credential theft and account use.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

**Observation:** The user confirms clicking the link and entering credentials.

**Hypothesis:** Credentials or an authenticated session may have been compromised.

**Confirmed Evidence:** Examples include an unauthorised successful sign-in/session, the user denying the activity, malicious OAuth/consent or mailbox activity, or other corroborating identity/cloud evidence.

- Original email, NetworkMessageId, sender, subject and recipients.
- URL/domain and redirect chain where safely available.
- Email authentication and Defender/email-security verdicts.
- Whether other users received or clicked the same message.
- User sign-in timeline, source, device/client and MFA/conditional-access context where available.
- New sessions/tokens or risky identity events available to the SOC.
- Mailbox rules, forwarding, OAuth consent/app activity or other post-login evidence where relevant.
- Endpoint/browser activity if the page also attempted downloads or execution.
- Escalation and authorised identity-containment actions.

### Tools / Logs

Defender for Office 365 / Defender XDR, Threat Explorer where licensed, EmailEvents and EmailUrlInfo, Entra sign-in/identity telemetry, Microsoft Sentinel, Defender for Endpoint, Proofpoint/Abnormal Security where deployed, proxy/DNS/browser logs and approved threat-intelligence services.

### Interview Takeaway

"If a user entered credentials into a phishing page, I treat it as potential identity compromise even if nothing downloaded. I correlate the email and click timeline with sign-in, MFA/session and post-login activity, scope other recipients, and escalate for authorised identity containment when the evidence supports compromise."

## Verification Notes

- Microsoft Learn states that SPF identifies valid sending sources, DKIM cryptographically signs outbound messages, and DMARC uses SPF/DKIM plus alignment with the visible From domain and a published policy.
- Microsoft documents that legitimate forwarding or message modification can cause authentication complications; therefore authentication failure is not by itself proof of malicious intent.
- Microsoft Defender XDR documents `EmailEvents` fields including `Timestamp`, `NetworkMessageId`, `SenderFromAddress`, `SenderMailFromAddress`, `SenderIPv4`, `RecipientEmailAddress`, `EmailDirection`, `DeliveryAction`, `DeliveryLocation`, `ThreatTypes` and `DetectionMethods`.
- Microsoft documents `EmailUrlInfo` as containing URLs from emails/attachments and `NetworkMessageId` for correlation with the associated email.
- Microsoft documents `EmailPostDeliveryEvents` as containing post-delivery actions, including manual remediation and ZAP actions, when Defender for Office 365 provides the data.
- MITRE ATT&CK identifies Spearphishing Link as T1566.002. Current detection guidance includes correlation of inbound malicious links with user navigation and subsequent process/download or identity activity.
- No authentication pass/fail result, high-volume sender pattern, user click, or isolated security alert is treated above as sufficient by itself to prove compromise.
