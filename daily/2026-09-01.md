# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-09-01

## Question 34

### Interview Question

What is the difference between a threat, a vulnerability and risk, and how would those concepts affect your prioritisation during a SOC investigation?

### Interview-Ready Answer

A **vulnerability** is a weakness in a system, process, control or implementation that could be exploited or triggered by a threat source. A **threat** is a circumstance or event with the potential to cause harm, including through unauthorised access, disclosure, modification, destruction or denial of service. **Risk** is the extent to which an organisation is threatened by a potential event and is typically considered in terms of both likelihood and adverse impact.

In a SOC environment, I would use those terms operationally rather than as isolated definitions. A vulnerability tells me where a weakness exists; the threat tells me what could exploit or trigger that weakness; risk helps me understand how urgently the situation matters to the organisation.

For example, an unpatched vulnerability on an isolated lab machine may be technically severe but operationally lower priority than the same vulnerability on an internet-facing production server supporting a critical service while exploitation is being observed. The vulnerability exists in both cases, but the threat exposure and potential impact are different.

As a Tier 1 analyst, I would not normally own enterprise risk acceptance or vulnerability remediation decisions, but I would use vulnerability, threat and business-context information to prioritise triage and explain why an alert may require escalation. I would distinguish the observation from the hypothesis: a vulnerability scan finding is evidence that a weakness may exist; an exploit attempt is evidence of threat activity; successful exploitation still requires supporting telemetry.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

A SIEM alert shows repeated exploit attempts against two servers affected by the same critical CVE.

Server A is an internal test server with no direct internet exposure. Server B is internet-facing, processes customer data and supports a business-critical application.

I would verify whether both assets are genuinely affected, identify the attack source and pattern, then correlate WAF/firewall, application and EDR telemetry. The same vulnerability may exist on both servers, but Server B carries higher operational risk because exposure and potential impact are greater. If endpoint or application evidence shows code execution or compromise on Server B, I would escalate urgently according to the incident playbook.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Whether the vulnerability genuinely applies to the asset
- Asset owner, business criticality and data sensitivity
- Internet exposure or reachable attack path
- Threat source and observed exploitation attempts
- CVE/vendor advisory and remediation status
- CISA KEV or other reliable exploitation context where relevant
- Existing compensating controls
- WAF, firewall, EDR and application evidence
- Evidence of successful exploitation versus attempted exploitation
- Scope across other affected assets
- Existing exception/risk-acceptance records where available
- Escalation and remediation owner

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Vulnerability scanner; asset inventory/CMDB; SIEM; EDR; firewall/WAF; application/server logs; threat-intelligence sources; CISA KEV where relevant; vendor advisories; ticketing/change-management records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

A vulnerability is a weakness, a threat is something that can cause harm or exploit that weakness, and risk reflects the likelihood and impact of the resulting adverse event. In a SOC investigation, I would combine technical evidence with exposure and business impact to prioritise correctly rather than relying on severity alone.

## Question 35

### Interview Question

What is a DDoS attack, and how would you investigate a suspected DDoS event as a Tier 1 SOC analyst without assuming that every traffic spike is malicious?

### Interview-Ready Answer

A **Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) attack** is an attempt to disrupt the availability of a service by overwhelming it with traffic or requests from multiple sources. DDoS can target different parts of the stack, including network and transport layers as well as application-layer services.

In a SOC environment, my first observation might simply be **a large increase in traffic, requests, connection attempts or resource consumption**. My hypothesis could be a DDoS attack, but I would also consider legitimate causes such as a marketing event, software release, customer surge, monitoring issue or misconfiguration.

I would establish whether the service is actually degraded, what traffic pattern changed, which protocols/endpoints are affected, whether the traffic comes from many sources, and whether the pattern looks like volumetric flooding, protocol abuse or application-layer request flooding. I would correlate network telemetry with load balancer/CDN/WAF/application-health evidence.

For example, a large number of SYN packets across many sources may suggest a transport-layer flood, while a surge of expensive HTTP requests against one endpoint may indicate an application-layer attack. But I would not diagnose solely from one metric. I would compare with the normal baseline, confirm user impact and look for corroborating telemetry.

As Tier 1, I would document scope and impact and activate the organisation's DDoS/network-security escalation path. Applying rate limits, WAF changes, upstream filtering, blackholing, traffic scrubbing or cloud-provider mitigations should be performed only by authorised network/security personnel or through an approved automated service.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Monitoring shows a public web service becoming slow while inbound request volume rises sharply from thousands of source addresses.

I would first confirm whether the service is degraded and whether the increase is real. Then I would inspect firewall, WAF/CDN, load-balancer and application telemetry. Suppose the evidence shows a large distributed increase in requests against a resource-intensive endpoint, rising latency and error rates, and no matching business event or planned campaign.

That combination supports a DDoS hypothesis. I would escalate quickly to the network/cloud or DDoS-response team with the affected service, start time, traffic pattern, source distribution, impacted endpoints and current business impact. If authorised mitigations reduce the attack traffic and service health recovers, I would continue monitoring for changes in attack method or recurrence.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Affected service/application and business impact
- Start time, duration and baseline comparison
- Traffic/request volume and rate
- Source-IP distribution and geography as context
- Destination IP, port, protocol and endpoint
- TCP flags/connection patterns where relevant
- WAF/CDN/load-balancer actions and request patterns
- Application latency, error rate and resource utilisation
- Whether legitimate marketing/releases/events explain the surge
- Firewall/IDS/IPS/NetFlow or packet evidence
- DNS availability and upstream-provider health
- Evidence of reflection/amplification where relevant
- Whether automated DDoS protections activated
- Escalation path and authorised mitigation owner
- Recovery and recurrence monitoring

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Firewall and IDS/IPS telemetry; NetFlow/network telemetry; Wireshark/PCAP where available; WAF/CDN/load-balancer logs; application performance/health monitoring; DNS telemetry; SIEM; cloud DDoS-protection services such as AWS Shield or equivalent where deployed; incident-management playbooks.

### Key Interview Takeaway

A DDoS attack aims to make a service unavailable by overwhelming it from multiple sources. As a Tier 1 analyst, I would confirm service impact, compare traffic with the baseline, identify the attack pattern, rule out legitimate surges, correlate network and application evidence, and escalate mitigation through the authorised DDoS/network-response process.

## Question 36

### Interview Question

What is SQL injection, and how would you investigate a web-security alert suggesting possible SQL injection against an application?

### Interview-Ready Answer

**SQL injection** is an injection vulnerability in which untrusted input is incorporated into a database query in a way that allows the input to alter the intended query logic or be interpreted as SQL commands. OWASP's current Top 10 places Injection at **A05:2025**, and OWASP continues to recommend separating data from commands through safe APIs and parameterised queries as the primary defence against SQL injection.

In a SOC environment, I would not treat a WAF alert containing suspicious SQL-like characters as proof that the database was compromised. My observation would be **a request matched a SQL-injection detection rule or pattern**. My hypotheses could include a real attack attempt, vulnerability scanning/security testing, malformed application traffic or a false/benign positive.

I would review the source, target URL/parameter, timestamp, HTTP method, WAF action, repetition and response behaviour. I would then correlate the request with application/server logs and, where authorised and available, database-security or database-audit evidence. The critical question is whether the request was only attempted and blocked, reached the application, caused errors, returned unusual data, or led to follow-on activity.

I would also check whether the application or endpoint is known to be vulnerable, whether the activity originated from an approved penetration test or scanner, and whether other hosts or parameters were targeted. As Tier 1, I would document and escalate suspected exploitation; changing application code, WAF production rules or database configuration would normally belong to application-security, engineering or platform teams unless the playbook explicitly authorises me.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The WAF generates repeated SQL-injection alerts against a customer-facing login endpoint from one external IP.

I would determine whether the requests were blocked, which parameter triggered the detection and whether the same source targeted other pages. I would correlate WAF timestamps with web-server/application logs. If the WAF blocked all requests and the application shows no abnormal database errors, data access or follow-on activity, the evidence may support an attempted but unsuccessful attack.

If the requests were allowed, the application begins returning unusual responses, database errors appear and sensitive-data access or suspicious application activity follows, I would escalate urgently as possible successful exploitation. If the source belongs to an approved security test during a documented window, that may instead be a benign positive.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Source IP and reputation/context
- Target hostname, URL/endpoint and parameter
- HTTP method and timestamp
- WAF rule/signature and recorded action
- Repetition, rate and breadth of targets
- HTTP status/response-size anomalies where available
- Whether requests reached the application
- Web-server/application errors
- Database audit/security events where available and authorised
- Evidence of unusual data access or response content
- Known vulnerability or recent application change
- Approved penetration-test/scanner activity
- Other affected users/hosts/endpoints
- EDR/server activity if application compromise is suspected
- Escalation and containment/remediation authority

### Relevant Tools / Logs

WAF/reverse-proxy logs; web-server and application logs; SIEM; IDS/IPS; EDR on the application server where deployed; database audit/security telemetry where available; vulnerability scanner; threat-intelligence context; change/penetration-test records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

SQL injection happens when untrusted input changes the intended database query. In a SOC investigation, I would validate the WAF alert, determine whether the request was blocked or reached the application, correlate application and database evidence, distinguish attempted exploitation from successful exploitation, and escalate remediation to the appropriate application-security or engineering team.

- NIST defines a vulnerability as a weakness in an information system, procedures, controls or implementation that could be exploited or triggered by a threat source.
- NIST defines threat as a circumstance or event with the potential to adversely impact operations, assets or individuals, including via unauthorised access, disclosure, modification, destruction or denial of service.
- NIST defines risk as the extent to which an entity is threatened by a potential event and typically as a function of adverse impact and likelihood.

- AWS security guidance defines DoS as making a site/application unavailable and DDoS as using multiple sources to overwhelm a target; AWS and other providers distinguish infrastructure/network/transport attacks from application-layer attacks.
- CISA documents reflection/amplification DDoS patterns using spoofed UDP source addresses and amplified responses.
- OWASP Top 10:2025 places Injection at A05:2025 and includes SQL injection in the category.
- OWASP SQL Injection Prevention guidance recommends prepared statements/parameterised queries as the primary defence and emphasises separating data from commands/queries.
- A WAF or IDS alert is detection evidence and does not by itself prove successful SQL injection; confirmation requires application/database/endpoint impact evidence.
