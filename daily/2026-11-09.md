# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-11-09

**Curriculum:** Questions 197–200 | AD CS operations, certificate request/issuance evidence, Defender XDR correlation, and ESC1 incident response

## Question 197

### Interview Question

Microsoft Defender for Identity raises a “Suspicious Active Directory Certificate Services (AD CS) certificate request” alert after a standard user requests an authentication-capable certificate for a privileged identity. What is AD CS in operational SOC terms, and how would you triage the alert without treating it as confirmed compromise?

### Interview-Ready Answer

AD CS is Microsoft’s public-key infrastructure role for issuing and managing certificates. In an enterprise, those certificates can authenticate users or computers, so a misconfigured template can turn a low-privileged enrollment right into a path to a privileged identity.

I would first preserve the Defender incident and identify the requester, requested identity, certificate template, certification authority, source device, time and any related alerts. I would then ask four separate questions: was a request submitted, was it approved and issued, was the request authorised, and was the resulting certificate used?

A Defender alert is a high-value detection lead, but it is not by itself proof that a certificate was issued or used. I would correlate CA Security events—4886 for a received request and 4887 for an approved and issued certificate—plus the CA database, template configuration, device telemetry and certificate-based Kerberos evidence. I would validate the change or business owner and check whether the template allows requester-supplied subject information, supports authentication and is broadly enrollable, the combination associated with ESC1 risk.

Within Tier 1 permissions, I would document the evidence, raise severity if a privileged identity was targeted or issuance/use is confirmed, and escalate to identity/PKI and incident response. I would not edit templates or revoke certificates unless the approved playbook explicitly authorises that action.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

A finance workstation requests a certificate whose Subject Alternative Name identifies a domain administrator. The user has no PKI administration role and the template owner confirms there is no approved enrollment. Event 4887 and the CA record show that the certificate was issued, but no certificate-based authentication is yet visible.

- **Observation:** A standard user requested a privileged authentication certificate and Defender flagged the request as suspicious.
- **Hypothesis:** An attacker or unauthorised user is abusing an ESC1-vulnerable template to impersonate a privileged account.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** CA issuance evidence and owner denial establish unauthorised certificate issuance. Certificate use and account takeover remain unconfirmed until authentication evidence is found.

The verdict is **high-severity true positive—unauthorised privileged certificate issued; subsequent use and wider impact require urgent scoping**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve the Defender incident, entities, timestamps and alert evidence.
2. Identify requester, requested subject/SAN, template, CA, request ID and source device.
3. Correlate events 4886, 4887 and 4888 where present with the CA database.
4. Validate the requester’s role, device ownership and an approved change or enrollment workflow.
5. Review template enrollment permissions, authentication purposes, subject supply and approval controls.
6. Check Defender for Identity posture findings and related AD CS alerts.
7. Hunt for certificate-based Kerberos/PKINIT activity and subsequent privileged actions.
8. Escalate for authorised certificate, identity, device and template containment.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Identity and Defender XDR, CA Security log and database, events 4886/4887/4888 where auditing is enabled, certificate template configuration, domain-controller/Kerberos telemetry, endpoint EDR, Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk, change records and incident case data.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Separate request, issuance, authorisation and use. An alert starts the investigation; CA and authentication evidence determine what actually happened.

## Question 198

### Interview Question

You receive two CA Security events with the same request ID: event 4886 shows a request by `CORP\\alex`, and event 4887 shows that the certificate was approved and issued. The recorded request identifies `administrator@corp.example` in the requested identity data. What can you conclude, and what still needs proof?

### Interview-Ready Answer

Event 4886 means the certification authority received a certificate request. Event 4887 means the CA approved the request and issued a certificate. Matching the request ID links the submission to the issuance. The requester-to-subject mismatch is highly suspicious when a standard user requests an authentication certificate for a privileged identity.

The pair proves receipt and issuance as recorded by the CA. It does not alone prove that the certificate was retrieved, that `alex` was compromised, that the template configuration was malicious, or that the certificate was used to obtain a Kerberos ticket or access a resource. The exact rendered fields can vary by Windows version and event format, so I would preserve the raw XML and CA database record rather than rely on one parser’s labels.

I would validate the template and business context, identify the certificate serial number and thumbprint from authoritative CA records, inspect the source device and related Defender evidence, and hunt for the first authentication performed with the certificate. I would treat the privileged identity and requester device as urgent scope items while keeping the facts and hypotheses separate.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The PKI owner confirms the template is intended only for users to request certificates for themselves. Its enrollment permissions are broad and it allows requester-supplied identity information. The request was issued outside the user’s working pattern from a device that also generated AD CS enumeration alerts.

- **Observation:** Events 4886 and 4887 share a request ID and show a standard requester obtaining a certificate for a privileged identity.
- **Hypothesis:** The requester’s session or device is abusing an ESC1 configuration to impersonate the administrator.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** The matching CA events, vulnerable template settings and owner validation confirm unauthorised issuance. Authentication or post-authentication impact still requires separate evidence.

The verdict is **high-severity true positive—confirmed ESC1-style unauthorised issuance, with certificate use not yet established**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve raw event XML, request ID, requester, subject/SAN data, template and timestamps.
2. Confirm that 4886 and 4887 were emitted by the expected CA and refer to the same request.
3. Query the CA database for disposition, serial number, thumbprint and issuance details.
4. Review template purposes, enrollment ACLs, subject-supply settings and approval requirements.
5. Validate the request with the user, manager, PKI owner and change record.
6. Correlate source device, process, network and identity alerts.
7. Hunt for PKINIT, ticket issuance and privileged directory or resource access.
8. Escalate for revocation and containment under the approved playbook.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Windows CA Security events 4886 and 4887, raw event XML, CA database/console, certificate template configuration, Defender for Identity/Endpoint, domain-controller Kerberos telemetry, Sentinel/Splunk, CMDB and change management.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Events 4886 and 4887 prove request and issuance, not use. Link them by request ID, then confirm authorisation and certificate-backed activity separately.

## Question 199

### Interview Question

Defender for Identity reports an ESC1-vulnerable certificate template, while Defender XDR also shows an AD CS certificate-request alert involving a privileged identity. How would you interpret and correlate these two signals, and what evidence would move the case from exposure to confirmed incident?

### Interview-Ready Answer

The posture assessment and the runtime alert answer different questions. The ESC1 assessment identifies a configuration path that may allow abuse: an authentication-capable template, broad enrollment and requester-controlled identity information. The alert identifies suspicious request activity. Neither signal alone proves that a privileged certificate was issued or used.

I would correlate on time, requester, target identity, template, CA, source device and Defender incident entities. I would then reconstruct the evidence chain: template exposure; request received; certificate issued or denied; certificate-based authentication; and privileged follow-on activity.

For request and issuance, I would use CA events 4886/4887/4888 and the CA database. For use, I would look for Defender’s certificate-usage/PKINIT alerts and domain-controller/Kerberos evidence, then correlate privileged actions such as directory replication, account changes or access to sensitive systems. Microsoft notes that alert names and availability depend on product configuration and enabled sensors, so absence of a Defender alert is not proof that activity did not occur.

The case becomes a confirmed security incident when evidence establishes unauthorised issuance, certificate use, or malicious follow-on behaviour. A vulnerable template with no abuse is a high-priority remediation finding; a request that was denied is attempted abuse; an issued privileged certificate is an incident even before use is observed.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The posture assessment identifies an ESC1 template. Defender links the source device to AD CS enumeration and a suspicious request. Event 4887 confirms issuance for a domain administrator, and a subsequent Defender alert indicates suspicious certificate usage over Kerberos. Directory telemetry then shows DCSync activity from the same attack chain.

- **Observation:** A vulnerable template and a suspicious request target a privileged identity.
- **Hypothesis:** The attacker is converting low-privileged access into certificate-backed domain access.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** CA issuance, PKINIT/certificate-use evidence and DCSync establish successful privilege escalation and domain compromise.

The verdict is **critical true positive—confirmed AD CS abuse with privileged certificate authentication and directory replication**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve assessment, alert, incident graph and all entity identifiers.
2. Confirm the template conditions that create ESC1 exposure.
3. Link the requester, target identity, template, CA, request ID and source device.
4. Determine whether the request was denied, pending or issued.
5. Identify serial number/thumbprint and certificate validity from CA records.
6. Hunt for PKINIT/certificate-use alerts and Kerberos tickets.
7. Review privileged directory, authentication and resource-access activity.
8. Check other requests against the same template and other affected identities/devices.
9. Escalate confirmed impact and telemetry gaps; do not treat a missing alert as exoneration.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Defender for Identity posture assessments and alerts, Defender XDR incident/evidence graph, CA events and database, domain-controller and Kerberos telemetry, Sentinel/Splunk, EDR process/network evidence, Active Directory audit logs and incident records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Posture shows opportunity; alerts show suspicious activity; CA and authentication evidence prove issuance and use. Correlate the chain rather than letting one signal carry the verdict.

## Question 200

### Interview Question

A user accepted an unsolicited remote-support session. The attacker enumerated AD CS, abused an ESC1 template to obtain a domain-administrator certificate, used PKINIT to obtain a Kerberos TGT and performed DCSync. How would you contain, scope, eradicate and support recovery as Tier 1?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would classify this as a critical identity incident with confirmed endpoint compromise, certificate-backed privileged authentication and directory credential theft.

I would immediately preserve the Defender incident, remote-session and endpoint process evidence, CA events/database records, issued certificate identifiers, Kerberos evidence and DCSync activity. I would escalate to the incident commander, identity/PKI, endpoint, Active Directory and business owners.

Under the approved playbook, authorised teams should isolate the initiating device, restrict compromised identities, revoke the unauthorised certificate, publish updated revocation information and verify that relying systems can retrieve it. Revocation and Kerberos containment are separate: revoking the certificate does not retroactively invalidate TGTs or service tickets already issued. Password reset or workstation reimage alone also does not revoke an issued certificate.

I would scope every certificate request and issuance tied to the requester, template, CA and source device; every certificate-backed authentication by the privileged identity; all DCSync activity; and subsequent access to systems or data. I would check containment exclusions because an excluded privileged identity can preserve the attacker’s path.

Eradication and recovery should remove unauthorised remote access and persistence, remediate the ESC1 template and excessive enrollment rights, revoke affected certificates, rotate compromised credentials under identity-team direction, rebuild compromised devices from trusted sources, review tickets/sessions, and validate CA auditing and Defender sensor coverage. Tier 1 supports this work with accurate evidence, tracking and verification; PKI and directory administrators perform privileged changes.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The response team isolates the workstation, restricts the affected identities, revokes the certificate and publishes a new CRL. Kerberos sessions are handled separately. The template is withdrawn until its enrollment and subject settings are corrected. Scoping finds one additional certificate issued through the same template and file-server access by the attacker before containment.

- **Observation:** Remote support led to ESC1 issuance, PKINIT and DCSync.
- **Hypothesis:** The attacker may retain certificate, ticket, credential or session-based access and may have reached sensitive data.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Endpoint, CA, PKINIT and directory-replication evidence establish domain-level compromise; file access confirms follow-on activity.

The verdict is **critical true positive—confirmed domain compromise through AD CS abuse, with identity, certificate, ticket and data-access scope requiring coordinated response**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Build a UTC timeline from the social-engineering contact through remote access, enrollment, PKINIT, DCSync and follow-on access.
2. Preserve CA events/database, serial/thumbprint, template, requester, subject/SAN and source device.
3. Record endpoint process lineage, remote-support session, files, commands and connections.
4. Escalate for authorised isolation, identity restriction, certificate revocation and CRL publication.
5. Track Kerberos tickets/sessions separately from certificate revocation.
6. Hunt all requests, issued certificates and authentications involving the template and identities.
7. Scope DCSync, account changes, privileged logons, service access and data access.
8. Review automated-response exclusions and assign verified manual actions where needed.
9. Support template hardening, credential rotation and trusted endpoint rebuilds.
10. Verify revocation distribution, audit coverage, sensor health and absence of recurring activity.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Defender for Identity, Defender for Endpoint and Defender XDR; CA Security events/database and CRLs; certificate template/ACL data; domain-controller Kerberos and directory-replication telemetry; Teams/remote-support records; Sentinel/Splunk; firewall/DNS/proxy/file-server logs; change management and incident records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Contain the device, identities, certificate and Kerberos access as separate but coordinated workstreams. A password reset or reimage does not neutralise an issued certificate or existing tickets.
