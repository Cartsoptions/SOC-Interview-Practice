# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-09-04

## Question 43

### Interview Question

What is XDR, and how is it different from EDR and SIEM? Explain how you would use all three during a Tier 1 SOC investigation rather than just defining the acronyms.

### Interview-Ready Answer

XDR, or Extended Detection and Response, brings together security signals from multiple security domains so analysts can investigate a broader attack story rather than looking at one control in isolation. Microsoft describes Defender XDR as automatically collecting, correlating and analysing signals, threats and alerts across the Microsoft 365 environment, including endpoints, email, applications and identities.

EDR is narrower: it focuses on endpoint behaviour and response. It gives me device-level evidence such as process activity, files, registry changes, user activity and network connections, depending on the product and configuration.

SIEM is broader in a different way. A SIEM ingests and analyses security telemetry across many technologies and vendors. Microsoft Sentinel, for example, can ingest data from Microsoft and non-Microsoft sources and provides SIEM plus SOAR capabilities.

So I would not describe the relationship as “XDR replaces SIEM” or “SIEM replaces EDR.” In many organisations they complement one another. XDR can correlate related signals across security domains into incidents, EDR gives deep endpoint evidence, and SIEM can correlate XDR evidence with wider sources such as firewalls, VPNs, Linux systems, custom applications, cloud platforms or third-party controls.

As a Tier 1 analyst, I would usually start from the alert or incident I am assigned, understand the affected entities and attack story, pivot into EDR for detailed endpoint evidence when a device is involved, and use the SIEM to broaden the timeline and correlate evidence across the rest of the environment. I would still validate the underlying evidence rather than accepting an XDR or SIEM incident title as proof of compromise.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Defender XDR creates an incident containing a phishing alert, a suspicious sign-in and an endpoint alert showing PowerShell activity on the same user's device.

I would use the XDR incident to understand the cross-domain story and impacted entities. I would pivot to Defender for Endpoint to inspect the process tree, command context, file changes and network connections. Then I would use Microsoft Sentinel or another SIEM to correlate the same user, device, IP addresses and domains with firewall, DNS, VPN or other organisational telemetry.

My observation is that multiple security controls detected related suspicious activity. My hypothesis is that the user and device may be compromised. Confirmation depends on evidence such as malicious process execution, credential misuse, persistence, command-and-control or other corroborating behaviour.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Incident and alert timeline
- Affected users, devices, mailboxes, applications and IPs
- Whether alerts are actually related or only temporally close
- EDR process tree, files, registry and network activity
- Email/phishing evidence where relevant
- Identity sign-in and MFA context
- SIEM correlation with firewall, VPN, DNS, proxy, Linux or third-party telemetry
- Scope across additional users/devices
- Automated findings and remediation status where available
- Observation versus hypothesis versus confirmed evidence
- Escalation and containment authority

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender XDR; Microsoft Defender for Endpoint or another EDR; Microsoft Sentinel, Splunk or Wazuh; Microsoft Entra sign-in logs; Defender for Office 365; firewall, VPN, DNS and proxy logs; Windows/Linux logs; incident-management records.

### Interview Takeaway

XDR correlates security signals across multiple domains, EDR gives deep endpoint visibility, and SIEM correlates telemetry across the wider environment. In a Tier 1 investigation I would use the XDR incident for the attack story, EDR for endpoint detail, and SIEM for broader cross-platform correlation, while validating the evidence before concluding compromise.

## Question 44

### Interview Question

What is SOAR, and how would you use Microsoft Sentinel automation rules and playbooks safely as a Tier 1 SOC analyst?

### Interview-Ready Answer

SOAR stands for Security Orchestration, Automation and Response. It is the use of automation to coordinate repeatable security workflows across tools and data sources. In Microsoft Sentinel, current SOAR capabilities include automation rules and playbooks.

Microsoft documents automation rules as a central way to automate incident and alert handling. Depending on the trigger and conditions, automation rules can perform actions such as assigning, tagging or closing incidents and can invoke playbooks. Sentinel playbooks are based on Azure Logic Apps and can automate workflows that integrate with Microsoft or third-party systems.

A current interview answer needs to reflect the 2026 workflow. Microsoft retired the legacy method of invoking alert-trigger playbooks directly from analytics rules in March 2026. The supported pattern is to invoke playbooks through automation rules. Microsoft also notes that Sentinel customers should plan for the Defender portal because after 31 March 2027 Sentinel will no longer be supported in the Azure portal.

As Tier 1, I would treat automation as a way to make response consistent and faster, not as permission to automate every disruptive action. Low-risk enrichment may be suitable for automation, such as adding context, assigning incidents or running an approved lookup. High-impact actions such as isolating a production server, disabling a privileged account or blocking a business-critical service should follow the organisation's approval model and playbook.

I would also review what automation actually did. An automatically closed, tagged or remediated incident can still require validation if the evidence or business impact is unclear.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

A Microsoft Sentinel phishing incident is created. An automation rule adds a phishing tag, assigns the incident to the appropriate queue and runs a playbook that enriches the sender domain and URL with approved threat-intelligence context.

The enrichment returns a high-confidence malicious result, but the user is an executive and the playbook does not have permission to disable the account or isolate the device automatically.

I would review the enriched evidence, email delivery and click activity, identity sign-ins and endpoint telemetry, then escalate or perform authorised response actions. The automation speeds up collection and routing; it does not remove the need for analyst judgement.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- What triggered the automation rule
- Rule conditions and incident/alert scope
- Which actions were executed automatically
- Which playbook ran and what systems it contacted
- Enrichment results and their source/confidence
- Whether any action changed incident owner, status, tags or severity
- Whether any remediation action was performed
- Failure or timeout evidence from the automation/playbook
- Duplicate or repeated automation effects
- Whether the automated conclusion matches raw telemetry
- Tier 1 approval authority for disruptive response
- Escalation path when automation fails or evidence is ambiguous

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Sentinel Automation; Sentinel incidents and analytics rules; Azure Logic Apps playbooks; Microsoft Defender portal; Defender XDR/Endpoint; Entra ID; Defender for Office 365; threat-intelligence services; ticketing/ITSM integrations; firewall/DNS/proxy evidence as relevant.

### Interview Takeaway

SOAR automates repeatable security workflows. In Microsoft Sentinel I would use automation rules to orchestrate incident or alert handling and approved playbooks to perform enrichment or response. I would verify what automation did and keep disruptive actions within the organisation's approval and Tier 1 authority model.

## Question 45

### Interview Question

What is the difference between an alert and an incident in Microsoft Defender XDR, and how would you prioritise and investigate a new incident in the queue?

### Interview-Ready Answer

In Microsoft Defender XDR, an alert is a signal produced by a threat-detection mechanism indicating suspicious or malicious activity. An incident is a container that groups related alerts and evidence into a broader attack story.

Microsoft Defender automatically correlates alerts from multiple sources into incidents and can continue to add related alerts or merge incidents as the attack story develops. The correlation is based on factors such as related entities, artefacts, timeframes and sequences of activity; the exact internal correlation logic is proprietary. That means I would use correlation as helpful context but still verify that the alerts truly belong to the same investigation.

For prioritisation, I would not simply open the oldest or highest-severity alert. I would consider severity, impacted asset criticality, user privilege, attack stage, scope, ransomware or other high-impact threat context, active attack-disruption signals and whether the activity is still ongoing. Microsoft's current Defender incident queue includes a Queue Assistant that assigns a priority score from 0 to 100 using multiple signals, including severity, asset criticality, MITRE techniques, alert rarity and high-profile threats. I would treat that score as a prioritisation aid rather than a substitute for analyst judgement.

Once I open the incident, I would review the summary, timeline, alerts, impacted entities and evidence, then decide what needs deeper investigation. Defender's Evidence and Response view can show supported events and suspicious entities with verdict and remediation status, but I would still confirm the underlying activity and business context.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Two incidents enter the queue at the same time.

Incident A is medium severity and affects a normal user workstation with a single blocked malicious URL alert.

Incident B is also medium severity but affects a privileged administrator account and contains correlated identity and endpoint alerts showing suspicious sign-in activity, PowerShell execution and access to multiple servers.

Even though the displayed severity is the same, I would prioritise Incident B because of the privileged identity, broader scope, cross-domain evidence and potential for lateral movement or high business impact.

I would then review its timeline and affected entities, validate the sign-in and endpoint activity, correlate with SIEM/network evidence, determine whether the attack is still active and escalate or contain according to the playbook.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Incident severity and priority score where available
- Why the incident was prioritised
- Impacted users/devices/mailboxes and asset criticality
- Privileged or high-value identities/assets
- Number and types of correlated alerts
- Incident timeline and attack progression
- Evidence and Response verdict/remediation status
- Whether alerts truly represent one attack story
- MITRE ATT&CK techniques as supporting context
- Active attack-disruption or automated-response evidence
- Whether suspicious activity is still ongoing
- Related SIEM, firewall, DNS, proxy, VPN and identity telemetry
- Business impact and scope
- Escalation/containment authority and documentation

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender XDR incident and alert queue; incident Summary and Evidence and Response views; Microsoft Sentinel; Defender for Endpoint; Defender for Identity; Defender for Office 365; Entra ID; firewall, DNS, proxy and VPN telemetry; incident-management/ticketing records.

### Interview Takeaway

An alert is an individual detection signal; an incident groups related alerts and evidence into an attack story. I would prioritise incidents using severity plus asset criticality, privilege, scope, attack progression and current activity, use Defender's priority information as an aid, then validate the evidence before escalating or responding.
