# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-11-04

**Curriculum:** Questions 185–188 | VPN and remote-access triage, Kerberoasting investigation with Windows Event 4769, Wireshark packet-evidence interpretation, and risk-based incident prioritisation and shift handover

## Question 185

### Interview Question

A user completes MFA and establishes a corporate VPN session from an unfamiliar residential IP. Minutes later, the assigned VPN address scans several internal systems over RDP and SMB. The user says they are working from home but denies scanning. Explain what a VPN does in this environment and how you would triage the alert.

### Interview-Ready Answer

A virtual private network creates an encrypted tunnel between a remote client and a VPN gateway. It can authenticate the user or device and apply access controls, but it does not make the user, endpoint or traffic inherently trustworthy. NIST notes that tunnel protection covers the path between the client and gateway; communications beyond the gateway and the security of the client device require other controls.

In a SOC environment, I would use the VPN logs to establish who authenticated, when, from which public address and device, what internal address was assigned, how MFA was satisfied, and when the session ended. I would then correlate that session with firewall, identity and endpoint evidence. The administrator or network-engineering team owns VPN policy, routing and gateway configuration; Tier 1 validates the alert, scopes activity, follows approved containment and escalates changes that could affect users or network availability.

A successful MFA-backed session is strong authentication evidence, but it is not proof of legitimate user intent. The account could be session-hijacked, the endpoint could be compromised, or the user could have approved an unexpected prompt. Conversely, a changed residential address can be normal. I would verify the device identity and posture, compare the location and access pattern with the user's baseline, review the VPN address mapping, and establish whether the RDP/SMB activity was a scan, an approved management tool or ordinary connection retries.

If the source mapping, timing and network telemetry link the authenticated session to unauthorised internal discovery, I would treat it as a suspected compromised remote-access session. Under the playbook I would revoke or request termination of the VPN/session, contain the account or device as authorised, preserve evidence and escalate. I would not change gateway policy or block a shared residential IP without assessing collateral impact.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The VPN gateway confirms that the same authenticated session received `10.20.30.45`. Internal firewall logs show that address attempting RDP and SMB connections to 120 hosts within three minutes. The managed device certificate is absent, EDR places the user's laptop offline during the session, and the user confirms that they did not initiate the activity.

- **Observation:** A successful MFA-backed VPN session is followed by high-rate internal RDP/SMB connection attempts from its assigned address.
- **Hypothesis:** An attacker is using a compromised credential, session or unmanaged endpoint to perform internal discovery through remote access.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Gateway address assignment, matching network timestamps, absent expected device identity, user denial and the device inconsistency establish unauthorised use of the VPN session.

The verdict is **high-severity true positive — confirmed unauthorised remote-access session with internal scanning; the original credential or session-compromise method remains unconfirmed**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve the VPN authentication, MFA, device, assigned-address, session-start and session-end records.
2. Confirm time synchronisation and map the public and assigned internal addresses to the exact session.
3. Review device certificate, managed-device identifier and posture/compliance result where the product records them.
4. Compare location, provider, time, device and access pattern with the user's baseline and travel/on-call context.
5. Correlate internal firewall, RDP, SMB, DNS, proxy and endpoint activity using the assigned address and time window.
6. Distinguish a connection sweep from approved vulnerability scanning, administration or software deployment.
7. Review Entra/AD sign-ins, MFA details, session changes and other activity by the identity.
8. Contact the user through a trusted channel without revealing unnecessary sensitive details.
9. Invoke authorised session revocation, account restriction or device containment under the playbook.
10. Escalate the evidence chain, affected targets, containment status and remaining uncertainty.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

VPN gateway and authentication logs, RADIUS/identity-provider and MFA evidence, device-certificate or posture records where available, internal firewall/IDS logs, RDP/SMB telemetry, Microsoft Entra or AD logs, Defender for Endpoint or another EDR, Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk, asset inventory and change records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

A VPN protects and controls remote connectivity; it does not prove that activity inside an authenticated tunnel is legitimate. I would correlate the session's identity, device and assigned address with internal behaviour before invoking authorised containment.

## Question 186

### Interview Question

Domain-controller Security logs show one workstation requesting service tickets for many different service principal names within two minutes. Many Event ID 4769 records use ticket encryption type `0x17`. How would you investigate possible Kerberoasting without treating every RC4 ticket as malicious?

### Interview-Ready Answer

Kerberoasting is an Active Directory credential-access technique in which an authenticated principal requests Kerberos service tickets for accounts associated with service principal names, then attempts to crack the ticket material offline. MITRE ATT&CK identifies this as T1558.003.

Windows Security Event ID 4769 is generated on domain controllers when the Key Distribution Center receives a Kerberos Ticket Granting Service request. Microsoft documents the requesting account, service name, client address, ticket encryption type, status and related fields. In the updated Event 4769 version introduced through January 2025-or-later security updates on Windows Server 2016 and later, additional encryption-capability and ticket-hash fields may be present. I would therefore preserve the raw XML and verify the domain-controller version and patch level before assuming every field exists.

In the event's hexadecimal representation, `0x17` corresponds to decimal 23, RC4-HMAC. RC4 is weaker and is being phased out, but Microsoft explains that legacy accounts or devices may still require it. A single RC4 service ticket is not proof of Kerberoasting. The stronger signal is a burst of unusual service-ticket requests from one account or host, especially for SPNs that the user does not normally access, combined with suspicious endpoint activity or later credential use.

I would baseline the requesting account, workstation, requested services and rate; identify whether an approved scanner, inventory tool or application generated the activity; and inspect EDR telemetry on the source. I would not claim that a password was cracked merely because tickets were requested. Confirmation requires additional evidence such as an unauthorised ticket-requesting tool, dumped ticket material, subsequent use of a service account, or specialist analysis that establishes credential compromise.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The account normally accesses one file service, but the workstation requests RC4 tickets for 43 unrelated service accounts in 90 seconds. Defender for Endpoint shows an unsigned tool enumerating SPNs and writing ticket files. No authorised assessment is scheduled, and a service account later authenticates from the same device.

- **Observation:** One workstation produces an abnormal burst of Event 4769 requests for many service accounts, predominantly with RC4 ticket encryption.
- **Hypothesis:** An authenticated attacker is collecting service tickets for offline password cracking.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Endpoint telemetry identifies unauthorised SPN enumeration and ticket export, and subsequent service-account use from the source device establishes credential abuse.

The verdict is **critical true positive — confirmed Kerberoasting activity followed by service-account compromise; the exact cracking method and any wider privilege escalation require further investigation**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve raw Event 4769 XML from the domain controllers and verify audit coverage, version and time synchronisation.
2. Review requesting account, client address, service name/SPN, status, ticket encryption type and timestamp.
3. Compare the request volume, service diversity and timing with the account and device baseline.
4. Identify whether requested SPNs belong to privileged or high-impact service accounts.
5. Check whether the environment legitimately depends on RC4 and whether AES-capable services normally use stronger encryption.
6. Correlate EDR process, command-line, file and user evidence on the requesting workstation.
7. Review successful logons and service-account use after the ticket-request burst.
8. Check authorised penetration tests, vulnerability scans, inventory jobs and application behaviour.
9. Hunt for the same requesting account, source host, SPNs, tools and follow-on logons elsewhere.
10. Invoke authorised host, identity and session containment and escalate to AD/IR specialists when evidence supports compromise.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Domain-controller Windows Security Event 4769 where Audit Kerberos Service Ticket Operations is enabled, Event 4624/4648 and other identity evidence as relevant, Defender for Endpoint or another EDR, Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk, AD service-account/SPN inventory, change records and authorised-testing records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

RC4 and Event 4769 are investigative signals, not a verdict. I would combine an abnormal ticket-request pattern with source-endpoint and follow-on identity evidence before confirming Kerberoasting or credential compromise.

## Question 187

### Interview Question

A packet capture from a workstation shows a DNS query for an unfamiliar domain, a successful answer, and a TLS connection to the returned address every 60 seconds. Wireshark cannot display the application payload. What can you conclude, and how would you investigate?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would separate what the packets prove from what they merely suggest. The capture can establish that the observed host sent a DNS query, received a response and formed a network connection at particular times. If the relevant TLS ClientHello is visible and not protected by mechanisms that conceal it, Wireshark may expose the Server Name Indication using the documented field `tls.handshake.extensions_server_name`. Wireshark documents `dns.qry.name` for a DNS query name. These are packet fields, not threat verdicts.

The recurring interval may be consistent with automated beaconing, but regular update checks, monitoring agents and many legitimate applications behave similarly. A connection to TCP 443 also does not prove HTTPS or malicious command and control. TLS normally prevents an analyst without appropriate session keys or endpoint visibility from reading encrypted application data. Even a visible server name can differ from the ultimate application context, and encrypted DNS or newer privacy features may reduce what is observable.

I would use display filters as starting pivots, for example:

```text
dns.qry.name == "example.invalid"
tls.handshake.extensions_server_name == "example.invalid"
ip.addr == 192.0.2.10 && tcp.port == 443
```

The domain and addresses above are documentation-only placeholders. I would replace them with the actual indicators and confirm that the fields exist in the capture. I would inspect timing, TCP/TLS handshakes, packet sizes, certificates where visible, destination changes and stream behaviour, then correlate the connection with EDR to identify the initiating process. Endpoint, proxy and DNS logs usually provide better attribution and longer context than a short packet capture.

I would not upload a sensitive capture to a public service. If policy permits enrichment, I would use approved domain, IP and certificate intelligence. Confirmation would require evidence such as an unauthorised process generating the traffic, malicious infrastructure, payload execution, or a repeated behaviour chain across hosts.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The capture shows a query and TLS connection every 60 seconds. EDR attributes each connection to an unsigned executable in the user's temporary directory, created by a malicious script. The same process and domain appear on three devices, and the software owner confirms that the binary is unauthorised.

- **Observation:** The workstation repeatedly resolves an unfamiliar domain and establishes periodic TLS connections to the returned infrastructure.
- **Hypothesis:** An automated process is beaconing to command-and-control infrastructure.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Process attribution, malicious creation chain, cross-device repetition, unauthorised binary and infrastructure intelligence establish command-and-control activity.

The verdict is **high-severity true positive — confirmed endpoint compromise with periodic TLS command-and-control behaviour; the encrypted application content and any transferred data remain unconfirmed**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Preserve the original capture, capture point, time range, interface and collection method.
2. Confirm source/destination addresses, DNS query/answer, TTL, timing and TCP/TLS handshake outcome.
3. Use documented Wireshark fields only when present in the capture and supported by the installed version.
4. Examine periodicity, packet sizes, connection duration, certificate and visible TLS metadata without over-interpreting them.
5. Correlate the destination and timestamp with EDR to identify the process, parent, user and command line.
6. Review resolver, proxy, firewall and endpoint history for longer-term context and other affected systems.
7. Compare the process and destination with approved software, update services and monitoring agents.
8. Enrich domains, addresses and certificates through approved services; avoid public upload of sensitive captures.
9. Invoke authorised endpoint/network containment when malicious execution or C2 is confirmed.
10. State explicitly what remains hidden by encryption, including commands, content and possible exfiltration.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Wireshark or `tshark`, packet-capture metadata, DNS resolver logs, firewall/proxy logs, Defender for Endpoint or another EDR, Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk, certificate and approved threat-intelligence sources, software inventory and change records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Packet capture can prove network relationships and timing, but encrypted recurring traffic is not automatically malicious. I would attribute it to a process and corroborate endpoint impact before confirming command and control.

## Question 188

### Interview Question

At shift change you have three alerts: a blocked phishing email sent to an executive, a high-CVSS vulnerability on an isolated test server with no exploitation evidence, and a medium-severity EDR alert showing confirmed malicious PowerShell with an active outbound connection on a standard user laptop. Which do you handle first, and how do you hand over the others?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would handle the EDR case first because the available evidence indicates active compromise and ongoing attacker communication. I would not rank incidents solely by alert label, executive status or CVSS score. Priority should reflect the event's confidence, stage, scope, time sensitivity, asset and data impact, business criticality, recoverability and available response resources.

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 says incident reports should be triaged and validated, then categorised and prioritised based on scope, likely impact, time-critical nature and resource availability. It also identifies asset criticality, functional impact, data impact, observed stage and recoverability as useful risk-evaluation factors. The EDR evidence has both higher confidence and greater immediacy than the other two items.

For the EDR case I would preserve the process tree, command, user, device, files and network evidence; invoke authorised device or session containment; hunt the same indicators and behaviour elsewhere; and escalate to Tier 2/IR with a UTC-normalised timeline.

I would not ignore the other alerts. I would verify that the phishing message was actually blocked for all recipients, check for related messages or prior delivery, and record whether anyone interacted. For the vulnerability I would confirm the affected asset and software, exposure, exploitability, compensating controls, scan freshness and remediation owner. CVSS measures technical severity characteristics; it does not by itself establish current exploitation or organisational risk.

The handover should make the next action unambiguous: confirmed facts, hypotheses, verdict/status, scope, impact, actions taken, evidence locations, owners, deadlines and outstanding questions. I would use the case-management system rather than relying on an informal verbal summary.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

The EDR process tree and network telemetry confirm malicious execution, so the laptop is isolated under the playbook and related hunting begins. Threat Explorer confirms that the executive email was quarantined before delivery and no related messages exist. The vulnerability scanner finding is current, but the test server has no external or production route and remediation is already scheduled.

- **Observation:** Three alerts have different labels and asset contexts, but only one contains evidence of active malicious execution and communication.
- **Hypothesis:** The endpoint compromise presents the most immediate risk of persistence, credential theft or spread.
- **Confirmed Evidence:** Correlated process and network evidence establishes active compromise; email controls establish prevention; asset and network evidence establish that the vulnerable test server is currently isolated.

The verdict is **priority one: confirmed active endpoint compromise; priority two: validate and close or continue hunting the blocked phishing campaign; priority three: track the isolated vulnerability through risk-based remediation, with reprioritisation if exposure or exploitation evidence changes**.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

1. Validate each alert's raw evidence, timestamp, coverage and current status.
2. Assess confidence, attack stage, scope, time sensitivity, asset/data impact and business context.
3. Preserve and contain the active endpoint compromise under the approved playbook.
4. Hunt the malicious PowerShell chain, destination, account and payload across other devices.
5. Confirm the phishing message's delivery action, recipients, related messages and user interaction.
6. Confirm the vulnerability's asset, version, reachability, exploitability, compensating controls and remediation owner.
7. Reprioritise immediately if the executive interacted, the message reached others, or exploitation evidence appears on the server.
8. Record every action and its result, including unsuccessful containment attempts.
9. Hand over confirmed facts, hypotheses, priority rationale, owners, deadlines and exact next steps.
10. Escalate capacity conflicts or business-impact decisions rather than silently leaving active risk unowned.

### Relevant Tools / Logs

Defender for Endpoint/Defender XDR or another EDR, Defender for Office 365/Proofpoint/Abnormal Security, Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk, vulnerability scanner and asset inventory, network-segmentation evidence, threat intelligence, change/remediation tickets and incident case-management records.

### Key Interview Takeaway

Priority is evidence- and risk-based. I would address confirmed active compromise first, validate the controls around the other alerts, and leave a structured handover that makes ownership and next actions explicit.

- NIST SP 800-46 Rev. 2 explains that VPN tunnelling protects confidentiality and integrity between the client and VPN gateway and can support authentication and access control, but does not protect communications beyond the gateway or data on the client device: https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/46/r2/final and https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/specialpublications/nist.sp.800-46r2.pdf
- Microsoft documents Audit Kerberos Service Ticket Operations and Event ID 4769 as domain-controller evidence for TGS requests. The updated event version introduced through January 2025-or-later security updates adds fields including supported/available encryption types and ticket hashes; the answer therefore qualifies field availability by OS and patch level: https://learn.microsoft.com/windows/security/threat-protection/auditing/audit-kerberos-service-ticket-operations and https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/windows/it-pro/windows-10/security/threat-protection/auditing/event-4769
- Microsoft documents RC4 dependencies, the move toward AES and the relationship between RC4 service tickets and Kerberoasting risk. Legitimate legacy RC4 use remains possible, so RC4 alone is not treated as a verdict: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/security/kerberos/detect-remediate-rc4-kerberos
- MITRE ATT&CK T1558.003 and detection strategy DET0157 describe Kerberoasting and recommend detecting anomalous Event 4769 patterns, RC4 tickets and unusual volumes/service-account targeting in context: https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1558/003/ and https://attack.mitre.org/detectionstrategies/DET0157/
- Wireshark's current display-filter reference documents `dns.qry.name` and `tls.handshake.extensions_server_name`. The filters in Question 187 are starting pivots and their applicability depends on capture contents and Wireshark version: https://www.wireshark.org/docs/dfref/d/dns.html and https://www.wireshark.org/docs/dfref/t/tls.html
- The simulated domain and addresses in Question 187 use reserved documentation namespaces/ranges and are not presented as malicious indicators: https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2606 and https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5737
- NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3, finalised in April 2025, says incident reports should be triaged and validated, incidents categorised and prioritised, and response urgency based on scope, likely impact, time-critical nature and resource availability. It also recommends preserving investigation records/evidence and validating incident magnitude: https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/61/r3/final and https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/specialpublications/nist.sp.800-61r3.pdf
