# SOC Interview Practice — 2026-08-24

## Question 16

### Interview Question

What is the Cyber Kill Chain, and how would you use it during a SOC investigation rather than just reciting its seven stages?

### Interview-Ready Answer

The Cyber Kill Chain is an intrusion-analysis model developed by Lockheed Martin. Its seven stages are Reconnaissance, Weaponization, Delivery, Exploitation, Installation, Command and Control, and Actions on Objectives. The model helps defenders understand where observed activity may fit in an intrusion and where defensive controls can detect, deny or disrupt the adversary.

In a SOC environment, I would not assume every alert cleanly proves a particular stage or that every modern attack will appear as seven perfectly visible events. I would start with telemetry, establish what is observed, and use the model to organise the attack story and identify defensive opportunities. For example, a phishing email is evidence around Delivery; a successful exploit would require evidence supporting Exploitation; a newly established malicious callback may support Command and Control.

As a Tier 1 analyst, I would use the Kill Chain to help structure triage and escalation, but I would use the underlying logs as evidence. I would not treat a framework label as proof of compromise or attribution.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

An email-security alert shows a malicious attachment delivered to a user. EDR later shows an unexpected process spawned from the document, followed by a persistence mechanism and outbound communication to attacker infrastructure. I would build a timeline from email delivery through execution, persistence and network activity. The Kill Chain can help describe how the evidence progresses across Delivery, Exploitation/Execution context, Installation and Command and Control, while the actual email, endpoint and network telemetry supports the conclusion.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Raw telemetry and timestamps before assigning a stage
- Initial access or delivery evidence such as email, web or removable media
- Evidence that code actually executed rather than assuming delivery equals exploitation
- Process tree, files, registry or persistence evidence
- DNS, proxy, firewall and EDR network activity for possible C2
- Identity activity and lateral movement where relevant
- Whether the adversary reached an objective such as data access, exfiltration or disruption
- Which controls detected or blocked the activity
- Other affected users or hosts
- Escalation and containment requirements in the playbook

### Tools / Logs

Email-security telemetry; Microsoft Defender for Endpoint or other EDR; Microsoft Sentinel, Splunk or Wazuh; Windows/Linux logs; DNS/proxy/firewall telemetry; identity logs; threat intelligence; incident-management records.

### Interview Takeaway

The Cyber Kill Chain is a seven-stage intrusion model. In a SOC investigation, I would use it to organise confirmed attacker behaviour and identify where controls detected or disrupted the activity, while relying on the underlying telemetry—not the framework label—to prove what happened.

## Question 17

### Interview Question

What is CVSS, and if two vulnerabilities both have a CVSS score of 9.8, would you automatically give them the same remediation priority?

### Interview-Ready Answer

CVSS, the Common Vulnerability Scoring System, is an open framework maintained by FIRST for communicating the characteristics and severity of software vulnerabilities. The current CVSS specification is version 4.0. CVSS v4.0 includes Base, Threat, Environmental and Supplemental metric groups, and its scoring system can produce a score from 0 to 10.

I would not automatically give two vulnerabilities the same remediation priority just because they have the same CVSS Base score. Severity is important, but operational priority also depends on context such as whether the affected asset is internet-facing, its business criticality, whether exploitation is occurring in the wild, available compensating controls, exposure, and the organisation's environment. CISA specifically recommends using its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog as an input to vulnerability-management prioritisation because those vulnerabilities have evidence of exploitation in the wild.

As a Tier 1 SOC analyst, I may not own patch deployment, but vulnerability context can change how urgently I investigate an alert. If suspicious activity targets an exposed system with a relevant vulnerability that is in CISA KEV, that increases concern; it still does not by itself prove successful exploitation.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

Two servers each have a vulnerability scored 9.8. Server A is an isolated internal test server with compensating controls and no evidence of exploitation. Server B is an internet-facing production server, supports a critical service, and the relevant CVE appears in CISA's KEV Catalog. I would expect Server B to receive substantially more urgent risk attention. If the SOC also sees exploitation attempts against Server B, I would correlate WAF/firewall, application and EDR evidence and escalate based on the combined evidence.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- CVE and CVSS version/vector rather than only the numeric score
- Affected asset and whether the finding is genuine/applicable
- Internet exposure and attack path
- Business criticality and data/service impact
- CISA KEV status or other reliable evidence of active exploitation
- Vendor advisories and available remediation
- Existing compensating controls
- Threat intelligence and observed exploitation attempts
- Patch/mitigation status and approved exception
- EDR, WAF, firewall or application evidence if exploitation is suspected
- Verification/rescan after remediation where applicable

### Tools / Logs

Vulnerability-management platform; FIRST CVSS resources; CISA KEV Catalog; vendor advisories; asset inventory/CMDB; SIEM; EDR; WAF/firewall/application logs; threat intelligence; change/ticketing systems.

### Interview Takeaway

CVSS communicates vulnerability severity; it is not a complete business-priority score. I would combine CVSS with asset exposure, criticality, active-exploitation evidence such as CISA KEV, compensating controls and observed attack activity before deciding how urgently a vulnerability should be handled.

## Question 18

### Interview Question

An EDR alert flags an unknown executable on a user's workstation. How would you triage the file as a Tier 1 SOC analyst without executing it?

### Interview-Ready Answer

I would begin with static and contextual evidence rather than executing an unknown file. My goal is to establish what the file is, how it arrived, whether it ran, what launched it, what it did, and whether the behaviour appears elsewhere in the environment.

I would collect the file path and name, cryptographic hash such as SHA-256, digital-signature information where available, creation/modification context, prevalence, parent/child process relationships and the user/device involved. I would use the EDR file/entity investigation capabilities and approved reputation or threat-intelligence sources to assess the hash and related indicators. A valid digital signature can increase confidence about publisher identity and file integrity, but it is not by itself proof that every observed behaviour is benign; similarly, an unknown or unsigned file is not automatically malware.

I would then correlate execution and behavioural evidence: command line, child processes, file or registry changes, persistence, DNS lookups and outbound connections. Microsoft Defender for Endpoint supports investigations of files, devices, users, IP addresses and domains and provides alert/entity context for this type of workflow.

As Tier 1, I would document findings and escalate according to the playbook when the evidence supports malicious or sufficiently suspicious activity. I would not detonate or run the unknown executable on a production endpoint simply to see what happens.

### Real-World SOC Scenario

EDR detects an executable launched from a user's Downloads directory shortly after a browser download. The file is uncommon in the organisation. I would inspect the process tree, hash, signature and source context, then check whether the process created files, changed persistence locations or contacted external infrastructure. If the file is unsigned and low-prevalence but shows no malicious behaviour, I would keep the assessment evidence-based and continue investigation. If it spawns PowerShell, establishes persistence and contacts suspicious infrastructure, the combined evidence would justify escalation and authorised containment.

### What I Would Check as a Tier 1 Analyst

- Device, user, file name and full path
- SHA-256 or other approved cryptographic hash
- Digital-signature status and signer where available
- File prevalence and first/last-seen context in EDR
- How the file arrived: browser, email, removable media or another process
- Parent and child processes and command-line context
- File-system and registry changes
- Persistence mechanisms
- DNS queries and outbound connections
- Reputation/threat-intelligence results from approved services
- Similar file or behaviour on other endpoints
- Whether the activity matches approved software or administration
- Playbook criteria for isolation, quarantine or escalation

### Tools / Logs

Microsoft Defender for Endpoint or another EDR; Microsoft Sentinel/Splunk/Wazuh; Windows process and security telemetry; Sysmon where deployed; PowerShell logs where relevant; DNS/proxy/firewall telemetry; approved reputation services such as VirusTotal; software inventory/change records.

### Interview Takeaway

For an unknown executable, I would not execute it on the user's machine. I would investigate its path, hash, signature, prevalence, process tree and resulting file, registry and network behaviour, correlate that evidence across EDR/SIEM and approved intelligence sources, and escalate based on what the file actually did.

## Verification Notes

- Lockheed Martin defines the Cyber Kill Chain as part of its Intelligence Driven Defense model and identifies seven stages: Reconnaissance, Weaponization, Delivery, Exploitation, Installation, Command and Control, and Actions on Objectives. Lockheed Martin's defensive material maps opportunities to detect, deny, disrupt, degrade or deceive activity across the chain.
- FIRST owns and maintains CVSS. Its current specification is CVSS v4.0, released 1 November 2023. CVSS v4.0 uses Base, Threat, Environmental and Supplemental metric groups; Base scores range from 0 to 10.
- CISA describes its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog as the authoritative source of vulnerabilities known to have been exploited in the wild and recommends using KEV as an input to vulnerability-management prioritisation.
- Microsoft Learn's Defender for Endpoint investigation material supports investigation of files and related entities including devices, users, IP addresses and domains. Defender alert/entity investigation is intended to help analysts understand relationships and overall impact.
- A framework mapping, CVSS score, file-signature state or reputation result is evidence/context, not standalone proof of compromise. The answers above therefore require correlation with the affected asset, behaviour and supporting telemetry.
